Why visualize — the process and the formats
🧭
01 · Orientation
Why learn this if AI can build the chart?
▼

Kristen Sosulski, author of Data Visualization Made Simple. Day 4 was meant to be Operations Analytics in the morning; Cohen missed class, so Data Viz moved up and we started around 11am.

She opened by asking why we need the class when AI can build a dashboard. The class's answers: you still have to check the data is right, and you have to speak the language to prompt for what you want. Her minimum bar is that you can instruct AI to build these graphics with a purpose for your audience. Generating the chart is the easy part. Knowing the audience, how much to show and whether you have the right data is the hard part.

  • Visualization used to be a nice-to-have tech skill you might hand an intern. It's now the lens we expect over data, one that surfaces an actionable insight. She cited Fortune projecting the industry at $34B by 2034.
  • We are the brokers between the data and the decision. A real-world phenomenon gets translated into data, then the data gets translated into a graphic. Mistakes can enter at both steps. Bad data she can't help with; the second translation she can.
  • Every graphic tells you what happened, what's happening or what might happen: a report on the past, a live view, or a pitch for the future.
  • We're spoiled by good interfaces already: an Apple Watch, the WHO pandemic dashboard, the Robinhood app.
The worksheet

The in-class worksheet is what you turn in by Dec 20, as evidence of participation. Download it as a Word doc and fill it in. It isn't meant to be a finished product, and skipped exercises or an incomplete one are okay.

02 · Storytelling
Two graphics walked through: Serena and MBA pay
▼

Serena Williams heat map. X is her career over time, Y is the four majors. Cells show the finals she played, those she didn't (red) and those she won (green). She has 23 majors but never a calendar-year Grand Slam, which would be a full green column. She did win four straight twice, 2002–03 and 2014–15. Sosulski prepped the story first and revealed the chart in stages rather than showing it all at once.

MBA pay equity (Wall Street Journal, 2022). A scatter plot with one dot per MBA program. The yellow line is equal pay; left of it women are paid more, right of it men are. Most dots sit to the right, so you see the point before anyone says it. At one school female graduates were paid almost 30% less.

The technique

Capture attention, lead toward the conclusion, and let the data reveal it. The graphic is your evidence.

03 · The Process
Audience → data → question, and the chart picks itself
▼
StageWhat to ask
AudiencePrior knowledge of the subject. Data and chart literacy (histogram ≠ bar chart; a box plot's center line is the median, not the average; outliers; correlation). Why they care. Level of expertise, manager vs entry level. Are they in the room? A report has to explain itself; a dashboard has to anticipate what they'll ask.
DataRelevant, and how much: too much confuses, too little loses context. What missing values mean (zero, none, an entry error, a left join). Can you trust it: source, how and when collected, why it was collected, sampling. Level of detail: keep the micro data, since you can roll up but not back down. Time series or spatial? Permission for human-subjects data.
QuestionCan this data answer it? Put the question in the chart's title. If the answer isn't visible in the chart, it's still a draft.

Once those three are settled, the chart is an easy decision: time series and change over time leaves a line, a sparkline or an animation. The goal is impact: influence stakeholders, guide decisions, surface insight.

Activities 1–2 — from a prompt, and from a chart

06 · Activity 1
Williamsburg household income, on the whiteboard
▼

Capstone teams had about 15 minutes to sketch how they'd show household income for Williamsburg, Brooklyn, choosing their own audience. Take a picture of the board for the worksheet. Her critique of the boards:

  • Name your unit of geography. What gets filled with color? Borough → ZIP → census tract → block. Three ZIP codes means only three shaded areas, and data may not exist at block level.
  • How is income enumerated? Sum, average or median. Median, because an average gets skewed by outliers.
  • A histogram isn't divided. Split bars by a category and it's a bar chart. Decide which you have.
  • A doughnut needs a KPI in the middle.
  • MLiQ's board (average income by marital status, age bands inside each): clustered bars were smarter than stacks, but five bars per category is dense. Also, "heat map" on geography is a filled map; in Tableau a heat map is a table of data.
The twist

You could have answered the literal question in a second: look up the median household income. Nobody wrote that number down, and rightly. Everyone instinctively put it in context. Sometimes the question isn't the right one. Find the whole number, break it down, then choose how to enumerate it by geography. Her example: census tracts shaded by median income, with a hover showing population, households, race breakdown and condo value.

07 · Activity 2
One graphic, three formats: the Gapminder lesson
▼

Fertility vs life expectancy. Four variables: life expectancy, children per woman, region (color), population (bubble size).

VersionWhat you get
StaticThe relationship, by region. No country, no change over time, no narrative. Like paper
InteractiveA slider and country hover. Maybe too much data for the message, and you write your own story: user-driven storytelling
Presenter (Hans Rosling)The same graphic with a crafted narrative. By far the most powerful

What Rosling does

  1. Explains the axes first.
  2. Points out the encodings by name: the big red bubble is China, the book's "big red bubble" rule (p. 249). He doesn't narrate 200 countries.
  3. Picks the window that carries the message: 1950 → 2050, not 1800.
  4. Annotates history on the timeline, such as the HIV epidemic.
  5. Runs into the future, stops, and pulls back to where we are, using a prediction to raise a question: will it happen?

She gave no title on purpose, and the class read it differently, which shows how much we each bring to a chart.

Choosing the chart

08 · Chart Chooser
Pick by purpose: every option she walked through
▼
PurposeOptions
Compare categoriesHorizontal bar, sorted descending, her favorite: instant max and min, label the ends. Vertical bar when you can't reorder (years). Clustered for two categories. Bullet graph for performance vs target
Part of a wholeDoughnut with a KPI in the center, her other favorite. Stacked bar only with interaction, since static forces subtraction. Sankey for flows. Treemap for hierarchy. A pie should represent 100%
Change over timeLine (4 or fewer series). Sparkline: bare-bones, doesn't start at zero, like a Robinhood intraday chart. Stacked area only with interaction
DistributionBox plot (center line = median), violin, histogram (equal bins), density. Good for exploring or a technical audience
MultivariateScatter, bubble (size = third variable), scatter matrix, heat map (a table of numbers shaded by value, as in election tables), radar, parallel coordinates, small multiples
GeographyFilled (choropleth) map by region. Point map from latitude and longitude; sizing the points as bubbles can skew the geography. Connection maps; isopleth maps not bound by borders, such as temperature
Reading the harder ones

Radar: think of a bicycle wheel. Each spoke is a column scaled min to max, each polygon is one row (Djokovic at the French Open: first-serve %, double faults, winners, errors). It can't hold much data, and you must explain it first. Parallel coordinates: same idea with horizontal lines, each an observation (an electric car across range, price and so on). Powerful, but check it on someone outside the field. Small multiples: nine little line charts instead of nine lines on one.

11 · Cheesemakers Review
The dashboard pitfalls she sees most
▼
Pitfall
Two years aggregated. Bullet graphs then mix targets for 2012 and 2013 and the chart is simply wrong. You might have met the gold cheddar target in 2012 and not see it combined. Fix: a year filter with the All option removed.
Pitfall
No coherent color scheme. Gradients plus extra colors are hard to decode. Fix: pick one light and one dark high-contrast color, or brand color + light gray, and stop deciding.
Pitfall
Missing legend or labels. Fix: worksheet down arrow on the dashboard › Legend; Label on the Marks card. With direct labels you don't need a y-axis or grid lines.
Pitfall
Doesn't fit one screen. Fix: plan ahead. Start at 1000 × 800 and use layout containers (horizontal and vertical objects): set up the boxes first, then drop in worksheets, filters, logos. Set every worksheet to Entire View.

Formatting and analysis are separate activities, and in many organizations separate roles. Design decisions shouldn't be made while you're wrestling the data.

12 · Activity 3
Filters, a set, and dynamic highlighting
▼

Year filter

  1. Drag Date to Detail on one worksheet. It must say YEAR, as a blue discrete pill.
  2. On the dashboard, that worksheet's down arrow › Filters › Year of Date. If the variable isn't on a worksheet, you can't reach it.
  3. The filter's arrow › Single Value (dropdown), then Customize › uncheck Show "All" Value.
  4. Arrow › Apply to Worksheets › All Using This Data Source. Make it floating to place it.

Chart as a quick filter

Click a worksheet on the dashboard and click the funnel icon. Clicking "both" now filters everything. Users need telling how this works.

Set: manual, permanent highlight

You can't click a bar and color it. Down arrow on State › Create › Set, check the state, then drag the set to Color and edit In and Out. For a hex code, it's the second option under color. A set is true/false; a group has categories.

Dynamic highlight: a calculated field

Right-click in the data pane › Create Calculated Field. Square-bracket orange = your variables; blue = functions.

IF SUM([Sale amount]) = WINDOW_MAX(SUM([Sale amount])) THEN "Max State" ELSEIF SUM([Sale amount]) = WINDOW_MIN(SUM([Sale amount])) THEN "Min State" ELSE "Neither" END

The trap

Drag it to Color and nothing happens until you click the delta on the pill and choose Compute Using › State. The max is whatever's in the window, so filters change it: she got Washington and Rhode Island, then Texas with a contact-method filter on. The manual's Fig 1.21: "Neither" gives Texas at $300,928. My Q2 bars are split by year, so click the "Neither" label, not a bar.

Screenshot the dashboard showing the filter and a highlight for the worksheet. Full code and steps: manual 1.1.5, pp 16–25.

Design — pitfalls, then the ten standards
🥧
13 · Bars + Pies
The rules for bars, bullet graphs, pies, treemaps, doughnuts
▼
ChartRules
BarsOne color; orange adds nothing if the value is labeled (Dona Wong's rule). Shade one bar lighter only to show a projection. Bar about twice the width of the gap, since the eye is drawn to white space; use Size. Baseline zero. No shadows, no 3D (a value of 79 read as 72 on the axis). Negatives needn't be red
Stacked barsLabel directly if you can; put the most important category on the bottom in its own color; else keep a y-axis
Horizontal barsSame data looks chaotic unsorted and clear sorted: hospice admissions by diagnosis, cancer at the top, HIV/AIDS at the bottom
Bullet graphText label, measure bar, target marker, quantitative scale, plus gray bands for poor / satisfactory / great. Lets you scan down a column rather than read a variance table
PieNo color needed: direct labels plus white space, no legend. Biggest wedge at 12:00 going clockwise, second biggest counterclockwise, like a fan, because the eye starts at the top. Small slices → make it a bar. Every pie can be a bar. Must add to 100%
TreemapHierarchy and proportion (region → country, by revenue). Boxes get unreadable, so not for print or presenting; nice on a dashboard. Algorithm by Ben Shneiderman
DoughnutKPI in the center. One or two colors. Consulting reports use them a lot. Her critique: the center number should be the main KPI, and the title must say which wedge it refers to

14 · Activity 4
Pitfalls the class named
▼

The worksheet asks for five. What went on the board:

  • Poor, irrelevant or missing title
  • Color overuse, or primary colors that turn the same shade of gray in black and white
  • Chart junk: unnecessary decoration and animation
  • Unformatted numbers, such as 1,500k
  • Missing legends, undefined encodings
  • No attribution: where's the data from?
  • Bad aspect ratio, too wide or squished
  • Data density: too many categories
  • Wrong chart type, such as a line chart across categories instead of time
  • A misplaced midpoint on a diverging scale

And charts not driven by data at all: triangles and trapezoids drawn in a design tool, where you can't compare the areas and color means nothing. A bubble chart with gray bubbles, cut-off labels and tiny font should have been a sorted horizontal bar. Tufte: above all else, show the data.

15 · Ten Standards
The ten design standards (book pp 124–125)
▼
#StandardWhat she stressed
1Chart formatDecide print, projected or web first
2ColorOnly where it marks a real difference; a yellow background and traffic lights make everything feel urgent. Avoid red + green: opposite on the wheel, identical in grayscale, culturally loaded. 8% of males and 0.5% of females have some color blindness. Pick a light and a dark
3Text and labelsLabel directly; add a description for print
4ReadabilityNo decorative fonts. Horizontal text; if a label won't fit, reorient or show less
5ScalesNatural increments (1, 2, 5, 10, 25). Axis max just above the data. Over 999k, use millions: nobody should have to say "1,200k"
6Data integrityLie factor = 1 (below)
7Chart junkTufte's term. Remove borders, effects and grid lines, especially once you've labeled directly. Maximize data-ink
8Data densityA blob of dots: color and filter the clusters. Ten series on a line chart: small multiples with the same scale, or a filter to compare two or three
9Data richnessShow enough for the story
10AttributionCredit the data source and yourself. You don't want someone interrupting to ask where it came from
Lie factor (Tufte)

Size of effect shown ÷ size of effect in the data; you want 1. New York Times 1978, fuel economy standards from 18 to 27.5 mpg: the data rose 53%, the drawn lines 783%, so the lie factor is 14.8. Bars must start at zero. A Fox News bar chart of 35% vs 39.6% with a non-zero base is the same pitfall.

16 · In Tableau
Applying the standards, plus the style guide and her AI test
▼
  • Numbers: right-click the axis › Format › choose units, currency, decimals. Almost anything you can click, you can right-click.
  • Annotate: right-click a mark › Annotate › Mark. Anything in angle brackets is a variable from the Insert menu, and Insert only holds what's on the Marks card.
  • Horizontal y-axis title (her hack): erase the axis title and use the sheet name to write it horizontally.
  • Hide clutter: Format › Lines for grid and zero lines, axis ticks to none; right-click › Hide Field Labels; uncheck Show Header.
  • Copy formatting: right-click the sheet tab › Copy Formatting, paste on another tab, even in another workbook. You get about 80% of it.
  • Workbook-wide: Format › Workbook. Defaults are 9pt light gray; change to black Arial and turn grid lines off.
  • Custom palette: Color › Edit › create custom palette from hex codes. The old way was editing an XML file.

Style guide

Decide font, size, weight, color and highlight color before analyzing, as organizations do (she showed the NYU Stern guide with its exact purples). Then the choice is already made.

Her AI experiment

She prompted Claude for a Tableau workbook following all her rules, with hex codes. The first files threw errors; the next used Tableau's default colors and ignored her hex codes; the data was made up, with a column named after the chart. It took about two hours of redirecting to reach real data and a usable categorical style guide for print, in Lesson 7. AI editing a workbook is a heavy task.

Critique of Lab 1.1.1

"How many customers purchased in the promo?" alone is just 162, no chart needed, so ask a broader question. Traps: a y-axis running to 1,500 means the chart used number of records, not purchases. Age came in as a measure, so change the data type and group it (20s–50s) to fix bad density. Rename a filter as a call to action. Show the y-axis or the bar labels, not both. Put source and your name on the dashboard.

The doughnut: two pies merged
▼

18 · Time Series
Designing line, sparkline and area charts
▼
  • Line charts: 4 or fewer series, line weight 3–4 points so the back row can see it, simple symbols. Use about two-thirds of the chart area. Label each series at its endpoint and you can drop the legend; lines that don't cross can share a color.
  • Baseline: a line needn't start at zero. Start a couple of intervals below the minimum and end a couple above the maximum. A stock that never was at zero looks flat on a zero axis, and then why show it?
  • Biased comparison (Dona Wong): two line charts side by side can make both stocks look like they doubled when one didn't. Keep the actual values but scale the axes proportionally: one runs 20→40, the other 100→200, each a doubling, so the slopes compare fairly.
  • Projections: dashed line, via a dual-axis chart with one regular series and one projected.
  • Static report: a vertical grid line can replace tick mark plus grid line and guide the eye.
  • Sparklines: no shown axes, not for precision. BBC's TV minutes by age, endpoints labeled. Dashboards use them and bullet charts a lot.
  • Area: one series or several, absolute or 100%. Works best interactive, since one series hides what's behind it.

Exam hooks — Day 4
▼
  • Process: audience → data → question. Title each chart with its question; no visible answer = draft.
  • Two translations: phenomenon → data → graphic. We're the brokers between data and decision.
  • Three formats: paper (direct labels, no hover), screen (filters, KPI + breakdown), projector (one chart, minimal text).
  • Same graphic, three experiences: static gives no story, interactive is user-driven, a presenter crafts the narrative. Rosling: axes first, name the encodings, choose the window, annotate, pull back.
  • Heat map = shaded table; filled map = shaded geography. Name the unit, and use the median, not the average.
  • Defaults: horizontal sorted bar; doughnut with a KPI center; line with 4 or fewer series; sparkline; bullet graph for targets. Stacked only with interaction.
  • Lie factor = effect shown ÷ effect in data = 1. NYT 1978: 783% ÷ 53% = 14.8. Bars start at zero, lines needn't.
  • Ten standards: format, color, text and labels, readability, scales, data integrity, chart junk, density, richness, attribution.
  • Color: one light + one dark. No red and green. 8% of males, 0.5% of females.
  • Tableau: blue discrete, green continuous, italic = Tableau-generated, Columns = X, Rows = Y, no Show Me. Dynamic highlight needs WINDOW_MAX + Compute Using. Doughnut = two pies on AVG(0) dual axis. Animation = Pages + Show History + Trails.
  • Never aggregate two years under a bullet graph: the targets mix.
Warm-up — a recap and a few critiques

22 · Recap
Gridlines, the “Other” bar, and the bubble chart again
▼

The class recapped Day 4: filters (including a custom one), selective and dynamic highlighting, a little animation, and encoding variables as the right data type. That last one comes back today with geography.

  • The bubble chart, done as well as it can be, still fails. Color only repeats what bubble size already shows, so it's a redundant encoding. A bar would be better.
  • Background images and piles of icons on a slide hurt readability even when the chart underneath is fine.
  • A good horizontal bar: the "Other" category sits at the bottom, not mixed in, and each bar has its value labeled at the end.
  • Time series: watch the scaling, and too many series means you end up labeling and coloring everything, which gets messy.
  • New York Times egg prices: the line is the color of the yolk, the scale is simple, horizontal gridlines run all the way across from the axis, and only the endpoint is labeled.
When do gridlines belong?

A classmate asked. Label bars directly and you need no y-axis and no gridlines. On a time series you don't label every point, so gridlines at natural intervals help the reader.

23 · Map Critique
What good and bad maps do
▼
Good
Insets for Alaska and Hawaii. Show all the geography. People leave them out because they're far from the continental US; we built insets in the pre-module lab.
Good
Small states labeled outside the map (Massachusetts, New Jersey) instead of crammed in. Abbreviations are a decision you make.

Dashboards — design, then Lab 7 with live data
📟
32 · Dashboard Design
What a dashboard is for, and why most fail
▼

As a business intelligence tool, a dashboard fits on one screen, answers "what's happening now?", and can be understood at a glance. Stephen Few set many of the standards (book p 101 cites him). Dashboards began in the 1980s as executive information systems; now people at every level get one, filtered by role.

  • Cadence: looked at weekly? hourly? Set by how often the data changes. If it isn't updating, nobody comes back.
  • How it's used: scan the big picture, zoom in (a mouse-over or a tooltip chart like Activity 8), link out to the details.
  • "Now" is scoped: year to date, this quarter, this month, yesterday.
  • What goes on it: top 10 lists (customers, products, complaints), best ads, open issues, KPIs. For the capstone: what are your two or three numbers?

Why they fail

The class: too much information, hard to filter, not today's priorities. It has to tell a story and be actionable, and "doing nothing is still an action". Data validation happens while building: if you don't trust it you won't act on it.

Pitfall
Beyond one screen. The important part is below the fold.
Pitfall
No context. Gauges instead of sparklines or bullet graphs.
Pitfall
A KPI with no evaluation. October units: 869. Good or bad? By how much?
Pitfall
Highlighting everything, so nothing stands out; clutter like a clock, or the '90s animated envelope; color overuse; a poor chart choice.
📐
33 · Dashboard Layout
Grid, KPIs with context, plan before you build
▼
  • The screen is a grid with limited real estate. Use a reasonable number of columns and rows. Most important top-left; a single KPI goes right up top.
  • Evaluate every number: against target, above or below, by how much (say +5% with an arrow). The hover can hold "vs yesterday".
  • Round and use units. No more precision than needed; the mouse-over has the rest.
  • Apples with apples: group related metrics and label the groups. Show the same measure the same way (not a line for apples and a gauge for oranges), and line them up.
  • She'll post dashboard style guides; the BBC's has templates: KPI up front, then charts that expand it by category, by time and by relationship.
Her checklist for your dashboards

A KPI. A way to filter or interact. The ten design principles. Published to Tableau Public as a live link, unless the data is proprietary.

The process

  1. Sketch the grid on paper first: title and logo, three KPIs, filters, the charts.
  2. Add the containers.
  3. Drop each sheet into its own "parking spot".
  4. Beautify: title, text, logo, then the filters last.
  5. Go back to every tooltip. Erase one you don't need; never leave "Day of Date".

Building the interface and building the components are separate activities. Use a parameter to show what's selected next to the filter, and make it big. Reminder from her slides: divergent palettes with many reds and blues read as three colors and fall apart in grayscale, so test.

Lab 7: connect a live stock API
▼

The EV Showdown: Tesla vs Rivian on live data. One chart (growth of $1,000), two KPI tiles (latest close, % change with ▲ or ▼), a Time Period filter, a "Showing:" line, the logo, published to Tableau Public. The arrows are Unicode characters, so they work across platforms and aren't graphics. Colors: dark red #A50021 and gold #F2B705, one dark and one light so they stay distinct in grayscale. Manual ch 7 / her site's Lab 7.

Setup

  1. A free Twelve Data account → API key. Keep it somewhere safe.
  2. REST API connector, a .taco from Tableau Exchange (it may ask you to log in with your NYU account) → Documents › My Tableau Repository › Connectors.
  3. Driver, a .jar from tableau.com/support/drivers → ~/Library/Tableau/Drivers on a Mac.
  4. Quit and reopen Tableau. Connect › To a Server › More › REST API should appear.

The connector isn't specific to Twelve Data; it works for any REST API, open data included. REST = representational state transfer.

How an API call works

  • An endpoint for a set of data, then parameters after the "?": symbol=TSLA&interval=1day…&apikey=….
  • Limits: her plan showed 800 credits and 8 calls a minute. Building the extract is one call; a refresh after the 4:00 close is another.
  • A key lasts while the account is active. For client work, use a separate key per project so billing stays separate.
  • Test the URL in a browser first (each refresh costs a credit). The JSON reply has a list called values: one dictionary per day with datetime, open, high, low, close, volume.

Connect

  1. REST API › paste the URL with your key › Response Format JSON › JSON Path $.values[*] › Sign In. Rename rest_table to TESLA.
  2. Add (+) › REST API again (don't reuse the first) › same URL with symbol=RIVN. Drag it next to TESLA, click OK on the warning, rename it RIVIAN.
  3. A relationship on date, not an inner join. An inner join keeps only matching dates; the stocks don't share an IPO date. The start date 2021-11-10 is Rivian's first trading day, which is why the lab's version still works.
  4. Save as .twb. Data › Refresh Data Source calls the API again.

Prepare

  1. Rename datetime to Date, spelled exactly; the later steps depend on the names.
  2. Prices arrive as text (ABC), because JSON puts quotes around everything. Change the type and the next call breaks. So cast instead: Tesla Close = FLOAT([close]), Rivian Close = FLOAT([close (rest_table1)]).
  3. Test: Date (exact) on Columns, Tesla Close on Rows gives a line. Put both on one chart with a dual axis or Measure Values; Rivian's prices are on a very different scale.

A classmate's takeaway: this is why you'd use Tableau. A static graphic is a heavy lift (the doughnut was ten steps); live data refreshes itself.

36 · Exploratory Graphics
Box plots, histograms and Anscombe’s quartet
▼

Box plots and histograms are for exploring: for you, or a technical audience. Not a broad one; there's too much to explain.

  • Box plot: high, low, median line, 25th and 75th percentiles; the box is the middle 50%.
  • In Tableau: one measure on Rows or Columns gives a single line. Analysis › uncheck Aggregate Measures, then Show Me › box plot (grayed out until you do). Each dot is an observation, so know the entity.
  • Several box plots: the same min and max (fix the axis; that's the little pin), equal spacing and layout.
  • Histogram: one continuous variable on Columns › Show Me › Histogram. Tableau makes a bin field (not italic, though Tableau made it) and a count.
  • Bin size is the interval width, not the number of bars: size 4 means 4–8, 8–12. The default (1.51 here) is usually best. Edit the bin field to change it; put the count on Label.
  • The summary card gives count, min, max, mean and median.
Anscombe's quartet (book p 14, Fig 1.10)

Four datasets with the same summary: x mean 9, x variance 11, y mean 7.5, the same correlation to three decimals. Plotted, they're nothing alike: one linear, one curved, one with a single outlier, one with stacked values plus an outlier. Summary statistics and graphics together reveal the structure.

Scatter plot and trend line

x on Columns, y on Rows gives one dot until you disaggregate; Circle mark. Analysis › Trend Lines › Show, then Edit: linear, logarithmic, exponential, power or polynomial, and you can force the y-intercept to zero. For exploration you'd usually take it off. Her workbook: "statistical data graphics" (.twbx, class files).

37 · Multivariate
Scatter matrix, small multiples, bubbles
▼

Scatter plot matrix

  • One row per city, one column per crime: do some crimes predict others? Lots of arsons when there are lots of robberies?
  • Build: the same variables on Rows and Columns, in the same order: every pair on one sheet. Add trend lines.
  • The diagonal is each variable against itself, a perfect correlation (larceny vs larceny). For a presentation, move it to a dashboard and cover the diagonal with boxes holding the variable names. R does all this automatically.
  • A LOESS curve follows the shape of the data rather than a straight regression line.
  • Present it like the Day 4 storytelling: show the messy big picture, then zoom into one plot (murders vs population). Good for exploring and comparing continuous data; overwhelming at first.

Small multiples

  • Group by a category and give each its own little plot. Good without animation, for time periods or categories, but exact values are harder to read.
  • Order them: a chart of shots of spirits per week per person of drinking age ran max to min, South Korea down to Indonesia (old data, around 2010).
  • Use the first chart as the legend, as a baby-names grid did for boys and girls, with annotations (Ariel after The Little Mermaid).

Bubble chart

  • A scatter plus size (continuous) plus color: four dimensions. But circle areas only allow rough comparison, and bubbles hide data.
  • Best practices: a visible size legend; selective labels (or label all); interactive or animated is best; halos, borders and transparency for overlaps.
  • The crime bubble: Massachusetts cities, normalized per 10,000 people, or Boston's population dominates.
🟥
38 · Heat Maps
Shaded tables, and a separate legend per variable
▼

A heat map is a table shaded by value. A single-color continuous gradient is good; labels in the cells are optional.

  • Birthday popularity (month × day): white borders separate cells of equal value, which would otherwise merge into one block; square marks; February 29 is rare. Not a Tableau default.
  • Fatal crashes involving alcohol (month × time of day, percentages, divergent): midnight to 4am is dark; 6am to noon is low.
  • Bad: temperature over time for every state, made in R, is unreadable; and similar colors collapse in grayscale.
The crime-data problem

City on Rows, Measure Names on Columns, Measure Values on Color. One gradient across every crime fails: larcenies far outnumber arsons, so one column is always dark, there are lots of zeros, and without borders the cells merge. Fix: a separate legend for each variable, each on its own scale. In her Massachusetts example burglaries ran 26 to 220; motor vehicle thefts and robberies stood out in Boston, larceny in Provincetown.

Best practices: make the scale work, decide on labels vs color only, and use a separate legend per variable when they're measured on different scales.

How people learn: Mayer’s multimedia learning theory
▼

Recap of what to know about an audience: data literacy, where they are when they receive it, why they care or should, prior knowledge.

  • As humans: attention (always competing with technology; close the laptops), distraction, subject knowledge, biases, physical limits.
  • Memory: she said we forget about half of what we learn within an hour, unless we put it into practice, connect new knowledge to old, and build mental models. Correct ones; wrong ones are very hard to undo.

Richard Mayer, multimedia learning theory

How we construct knowledge from words and pictures; the foundation of her learning-science lab. We have dual channels (eyes and ears), each with limited capacity, and learning is active processing: attending to relevant information, organizing it into a mental representation, integrating it with what we know. Split attention (a phone in a meeting) disrupts it. The book quotes Mayer in ch 6.

43 · Storytelling
Present like McCandless and the New York Times
▼

Voice plus images, not voice plus on-screen text. We read faster than a presenter talks, so text on the slide competes with the speaker and lowers retention. Images with narration spare both channels. Bullet points also make you say more, to re-explain them.

  • A Stern management colleague's video: Gallup's confidence in institutions has small business second from the top and large corporations near the bottom, just above Congress; small businesses generate almost half of US GDP. Her narration matches what's on screen, with progressive reveal, annotation and highlighting of whatever she mentions.
  • David McCandless (TED): he shows a chart with no labels and asks what rises twice a year, at Easter and two weeks before Christmas, peaks on Mondays and flattens in summer. He repeats the guesses (chocolate, shopping, sick leave), then shows only the title and waits while people laugh: 10,000 Facebook status updates mentioning a breakup, lowest on Christmas Day. Then he scrolls through it with annotations. A stacked area because a TED room is dark and thin lines vanish. Steal this.
  • New York Times, Usain Bolt: every Olympic 100-meter medalist racing him, comparing with measures you can feel, with sound at the end so you hear the gap. They'd started from a flat chart.

Engagement: use their prior knowledge, ask questions, personalize it (ask them something ahead of time).

44 · Slides
PowerPoint pet peeves, and her quick fixes
▼

The class's pet peeves: reading the slides; misalignment; information overload; slide junk; jarring transitions; too long on one topic, then rushing ("not this slide"); turning your back; small font (her number one); charts with no explanation; bullets that already say everything.

Slides can support a talk, serve as talking points, or be a handout. Design for the first.

Quick fixes (book ch 7)

  • 16:9 widescreen, not 4:3; the black bars shrink everything (book p 153).
  • Show full screen; cut the text.
  • 1–6–6: one idea per slide, six lines, six words a line. She credited Seth Godin; the book credits John Zimmer (2010), p 159.
  • Presenter view: next slide, timer and notes (book p 164).
  • Match the slide background to the chart's. A white chart on a blue slide adds noise; it should look built in.
  • Explain your charts.
  • Images: keep the aspect ratio locked so nothing gets squished.
  • Tableau to slides: a dashboard at 1600 × 900, stretch the sheet, export a PNG. Set fonts at 16–20 in Tableau, not its default 9pt, which stays tiny however much you stretch.

45 · Post-Module
The video, the dashboard and the paper report
▼

The pre-module was reps; the post-module brings it together. The big takeaway is format: design for one format and reuse it everywhere, and it works for none.

Team (capstone)

  • A dashboard of your capstone KPIs; use its individual graphics across a narrative in a video under three minutes, in presentation mode.
  • A peer assessment, three questions per teammate (a tool her lab vibe-coded).
  • Graded on how well you use data graphics to deliver the message and insight, not your technical Tableau skill. The dashboard shows how you surfaced the insight; the presentation shows how you communicate it with your voice.

Making the video

  1. Export the slides as images and put them in order.
  2. Write a script or talking points; record the narration.
  3. Lay the narration over the images, edit, publish. Or record live over the slides, then still edit.
  • Tools: ScreenFlow (her Mac favorite), Camtasia, QuickTime, NYU Stream, Captivate, Premiere, PowerPoint's recorder.
  • Submit a link, not a video file: YouTube or NYU Stream, private but shareable.
  • Tell a story, not "on slide one we created this data graphic…". Don't read the slides.
  • A talking head distracts (research says so); cutting to your face is fine.
  • AI voice: she first said fine, then took it back. Would you give it to a client? If not, don't.
  • Keep one style guide and palette across all the graphics; switching from gray to color is jarring.

Examples: a 2012 team video on HIV in South Africa (strong narrative, ran over time) and a less polished one (flat images, lots of text, poor audio, though the music helped move it along).

Individual (paper report)

Anything unrelated to the capstone: find data or call an API. A full-page report with a narrative that references the graphics, or make it feel like a news article. A chance to try a chart you want to challenge yourself with. Tableau or R, whatever works.

AI on the post-module

She'd prefer the graphics weren't generated by AI, but she isn't hunting for it; the assignments are about how effective the graphics are in their medium. The course rule stands: AI is allowed and must be cited.

Reference · Day 5
Exam hooks — Day 5
▼
  • Geography: exact address → lat/long beats ZIP, city, county, state, country. Italic generated lat/long = the center of a category, not your data. Filled map enumerates; point map needs geocoding.
  • Geocoding: address → coordinates. Rooftop beats range interpolation. Geocodio for small jobs, Google Cloud for big ones.
  • Maps: insets for Alaska and Hawaii; the same scale across insets and paired maps; one encoding per map; define missing data; run the grayscale test.
  • Scales: continuous, stepped, divergent. Divergent only when most values sit mid-range.
  • Projections: Mercator (default) distorts toward the poles; Albers for US government data; equal-rectangular for world themes; orthographic for routes.
  • Cumulative data: filter to the last day and sanity-check against population (2.8 billion wrong, 9.35 million right).
  • Normalize: SUM([Cases])/SUM([Population])*100000. Per 100K, California gives way to Rhode Island and Alaska.
  • Dashboards (Few): one screen, what's happening now, at a glance. Evaluate every KPI; top-left; sketch → containers → components → beautify.
  • Lab 7: FLOAT() the text prices; relationship, not inner join; LOOKUP(…, FIRST()); LAST() = 0; compute using Date.
  • EDA: box plots need Aggregate Measures off; bin size = interval width; Anscombe: same stats, different pictures.
  • Heat maps: separate legends per variable, set before any formatting.
  • Perception: preattentive attributes; the dolphin in the rose; voice + images, not voice + text; 1–6–6.
  • Post-module: graded on message, not Tableau skill. Video under three minutes, a link not a file.
