02 · Zachary’s Karate Club
A min cut predicted who left with whom
▼

Zachary, an anthropologist, started studying a karate club around 1970 and watched it for three years: who went for a drink with whom, who seemed to interact a lot. He mapped 78 friendships among 34 members by hand.

Mid-study the owner (also an instructor) and a part-time instructor fell out, he thinks over fees. The part-timer started his own club and took members with him.

  • Vocabulary: this kind of graph isn't an x–y chart. Circles are nodes, lines are edges. Network science studies graphs and ties them back to something real: flights between cities, how a company is organised, contagion risk in the financial system.
  • Min cut: split the graph into two pieces by cutting as few edges as possible. He named the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm from operations research (the recording heard it as "four full percent"). It isn't the best way to find communities, but it's common.
  • The cut predicted every member's choice except one. The class guessed nodes 3 and 9. It was node 9, three weeks from his black belt. Switching meant starting over as a brown belt, so he stayed. The social structure said move; his individual incentive won.
  • Luca's point: in an organisational network you'd ask a different question, such as who bridges the two groups.
Takeaway

Math on the pattern of connections can reveal real behaviour, and twenty years in, he still looks for the network in every dataset he analyses.

03 · Political Blogs
Echo chambers before algorithms (2003 data)
▼

A study published around 2004 on data from 2003, by a researcher then at the University of Michigan who later joined Facebook. This was before Facebook and Twitter, five or six years before Google personalised search, and well before social media advertising.

Tie strength — close vs not close
🤝
06 · Strong vs Weak Ties
What makes a tie close
▼

(Everyone stood up for 45 seconds first.) Why a binary close / not-close split in an age of precise data? Because analytics only becomes useful inside a social, economic or political context, and sociologists found that ties between people really do fall loosely into strong and weak.

What the class said separates them:

  • Frequency of communication
  • Trust
  • Weak ties are often functional or purpose-driven: a coworker, a manager
  • Volume and depth of what you've shared, how much you've opened up
  • Emotional affect: what happens to you affects them, and the reverse
  • How long you've known them

Exercise: write down your closest friends. Most of the room had a small list; very few had 25 or more. This is the same prompt the study's subjects got.

07 · Facebook Tie-Strength Study
The data point is an edge
▼

Scientists at Facebook, roughly eight or nine years after it launched (he put the study at 2011), asked volunteers to list their closest friends.

  • Those Facebook friends were labelled strong; every other Facebook friend of that subject was labelled weak. If Sanchit named three people, three of his Facebook friends are strong and the rest are weak.
  • So each edge (subject ↔ friend) is a data point with a binary label and a set of features, the same labels-and-features setup as machine learning.
  • Features: comments, messages, wall posts, likes, photo tags (appearing in the same photo, whoever tagged it), pokes, plus attributes such as family, event invites, shared gender identity, age difference, same employer, same school and shared groups.

Aside: he joined Facebook in 2005 because his undergrads were on it. By 2008–09 the norm that students and professors don't connect there had settled. LinkedIn is different. Connect with him there (send a note saying you're in his MSBA class), because his main use of it is introducing students to people.

🔎
08 · Summarise First
Look at the data before the heavy machinery
▼

The first step in any study, network or not: summary statistics. Means and standard errors for close vs not-close friends, and eyeball the differences. Don't just toss everything into a model.

  • Correlation of the strong/weak label with each variable (he thinks it was a rank correlation): comments highly correlated; age difference negatively correlated; gender identity, employer, same school and groups only weakly.
  • Then ask whether each one makes sense. Photo tags are proof of a real-world relationship: you were in the same physical place. Wall posts might really be measuring how long you've been friends, since people wall-posted a lot in 2005–06 and hardly at all by 2011.
  • Sometimes the goal is a black-box prediction (which customers to advertise to). Often it's to explain and decide, and then the variables have to make sense.
Data ≠ reality

You have a sample, not the population. There's bias, measurement error, noise and missing data, so the truth in your dataset isn't the truth in the world.

09 · Logistic Regression
Which interactions predict a close friend
▼

Rule of thumb: if a coefficient is roughly double its standard error or more, it's statistically significant at 5% (a t-score of about 2).

VariableResult as he read it
Comments, messages, wall postsSignificant: they predict close friendship
LikesNot predictive. You throw likes at everybody.
Family membersPredictive of strong ties
Event invitesNot significant
Shared gender identityPositive: more likely a strong tie
AgeDoesn't seem to matter
Same employerMatters, but not much
Same schoolDoesn't matter
Lucas: why is the comments coefficient small if it's the strongest signal?

Scale. Comments can move by tens (up to around a hundred), while "same employer" can only move from 0 to 1. A variable with big swings gets a small coefficient for the same effect on the 0–1 probability. How the study normalised the variables matters, and he hadn't re-checked that.

🎯
10 · Prediction
Flatten the network into a table
▼
  • Second part of the study: predict whether each edge is strong or weak. They tried logistic regression, a support vector machine (SVM) and a decision tree.
  • Why not unsupervised clustering? There's a labelled ground truth, so it's binary classification. They took the network out and made a flat dataset: every edge is a row with a label and features.
  • Confusion matrix, with positive = strong tie. False positive (type I, top-right) = predicted strong, actually weak. False negative (type II, bottom-left) = predicted weak, actually strong.
  • False negatives far outnumbered false positives. That sets up the threshold discussion after the break.

"Reality" here means the data's labels, which is separate from the reality of the population.

Bias and sampling
🪤
11 · Bias
Population vs sample
▼

You're almost never looking at all the data. Even a model trained on everything humans have written hasn't seen everything that could have been written. The population is what you're trying to study; the sample is the data you have. Always ask what your sources of bias are.

BiasWhat it isHis example
SelectionThe sample isn't the populationMore social people join Facebook. Zachary may have missed quiet friendships and over-counted people who are loud about theirs. Online reviews over-represent the tails: furious or thrilled.
VolunteerThe sample selects itselfThe people who agree to a Facebook study may be more social or more open.
ExclusionThe analyst drops part of the dataDropping incomplete surveys drops people who dislike surveys.
SurvivorshipOnly the survivors are in the dataCustomers who stayed a year leave out the churners. Abraham Wald and the warplanes: the army wanted to armour where returning planes had holes; the planes that didn't come back were hit in the engine.
Hawthorne effectPeople act differently when observedPerformative reviews. You'd have listed different friends if the list were read aloud.
❄️
12 · Network Sampling
Why random samples fail for networks
▼
  1. Mixed relationship types. Digital traces lump everything into one kind of edge. His LinkedIn mixes former students, strangers and high-school friends; a Facebook friend means many things.
  2. Missing edges. Colleagues he sees every day aren't LinkedIn connections, because he doesn't need them to be.
  3. Random sampling breaks. A poll takes a couple of thousand random people. Take 2,000 random Americans and there will be almost no friendships between them, and you'd miss most of the ones there are.
Snowball sampling

Start with one person, add all their friends, then all of their friends (a two-step snowball). Then start again from another random person. Network sampling is a whole field; this is the common approach.

Thresholds — false positives vs false negatives
🧪
13 · Thresholds
Same test, different threshold
▼

The puzzle he left over the break: US COVID tests rarely gave false positives but often gave false negatives (negative today, positive tomorrow). In China it was the other way round, with similar technology.

  • Class answers: in the US a false positive hurts the economy, in China a false negative hurts the party (Luca); gentler swabs in the US; testing volume.
  • His answer: assume identical tests. A test measures something and calls it positive past a threshold. China set a lower threshold (more false positives), the US a higher one (more false negatives).
  • Why: the relative cost of a false negative vs a false positive. A missed case costs more in China (density; collective good over the individual). A false alarm costs more in the US (individual freedom; people staying home hurts the economy). He gave this as a common theory, not a settled fact.
📉
14 · ROC Curve
The curve is the model; the threshold is the decision
▼
  • The true positive rate and the false positive rate are measured separately. The false negative rate is 1 − the true positive rate.
  • The ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve is a property of the test or model. Where you sit on it is set by the threshold. The US and China could share one curve and sit at opposite ends.
  • Extremes: a test that always says positive has a zero false negative rate and a very high false positive rate; always negative is the reverse.
  • If anyone tells you "based on the ROC curve, operate here," question it. The right point depends on the cost of a false positive vs a false negative.
  • Some models are simply better: their curve sits above the other everywhere. Pick the highest AUC (area under the curve), unless the curves cross and you'd be operating where the higher-AUC model is worse.
💸
15 · Cost Asymmetry
Venture capital and dating
▼

Early-stage venture capital (VC). A fund of, say, $200M (a few hundred million is typical) charges around 2% a year in fees, so $4M a year. It makes about 25 investments. The expectation: about 20 go to zero, the hope is that the other five return 2–3x, and that one is the fund returner at 25–50x.

  • False positive = invest in a company that fails: cheap, it's expected.
  • False negative = pass on the winner: very costly.
  • No early-stage VC he's met regrets a failed investment. Every regret is a company they passed on. A mutual fund or pension fund has a very different trade-off, so even with the same analysis machine they'd operate at a different point.

Dating. Finding your soulmate on a date = true positive. Skipping someone who wasn't = true negative. A wasted evening = false positive. Skipping your soulmate = false negative. How often you say yes reflects how you weigh those costs. One student once said the costliest error is the true positive.

Triadic closure, weak ties, structural holes
🔺
16 · Triadic Closure
A simplified model of how friendships close
▼

What a social scientist means by a model: a simplified representation of a complex reality, not a fitted machine-learning model. You can't model the economy perfectly without modelling every person in it. You know the model is wrong; you use it for direction and magnitude. That's why the Fed moves rates by a quarter point, not 0.37.

Triadic closure (Granovetter): if A is close to B and A is close to C, it's highly likely B and C have at least a weak tie. Why, from the class:

  • More opportunity to meet each other
  • It's easier for A: one shared relationship instead of two parallel ones, and no having to choose sides if B and C fight
  • B and C are both close to A, so they likely have things in common
The counterexample

His daughter in middle school: she wanted her friends to be friends through her, so she'd be the broker. It passed by high school. The business version: the colleague who wants every introduction to go through them.

Strong triadic closure: in the model, turn "highly likely" into "always". If A has strong ties to B and C, B and C have at least a weak tie.

🌉
17 · Local Bridges
Manhattan has no bridges, only local bridges
▼
  • Bridge: remove it and the graph splits. An island with exactly one bridge to the mainland.
  • Local bridge: an edge whose two endpoints share no neighbour. Removing it doesn't disconnect anything; it just makes distances much longer. Close the Williamsburg Bridge and you take the Manhattan Bridge or the Holland Tunnel, with a longer commute.
  • Add edges until every pair shares a neighbour and there are no local bridges left.
  • Local bridges connect otherwise distant parts of the network. They bring Brooklyn closer to Manhattan.
🧩
18 · Strength of Weak Ties
Local bridges are weak ties
▼
The theorem

If strong triadic closure holds and every node has at least two strong ties, then every local bridge is a weak tie.

Proof. Say A–B is a local bridge and it's strong. A has another strong tie, A–E. By strong triadic closure, E and B must be connected. Now A and B share a neighbour (E), so A–B isn't a local bridge. Contradiction.

Real world: closure isn't 100%, but it's highly likely, so local bridges are likely to be weak ties.

  • Granovetter's original question was how people hear about jobs. Most heard through an acquaintance, not a close friend.
  • Why: strong ties put you in a dense cluster where everyone has the same information. If you hear something new, it probably came from a distant part of the network, and the ties reaching there are weak ties.
  • The same holds in organisations: novel ideas and early warnings tend to come from another part of the company, not the people you work with every day.
🕳️
19 · Structural Holes
Broker or centre: which position wins
▼

Real networks aren't the neat grid you'd draw. They're dense islands with vast empty space between, and triadic closure is one reason they cluster. Dunbar put the typical person at about 150 friends and acquaintances, out of billions you could know. (His 7,000 LinkedIn connections aren't real.) The gaps where you'd expect edges are structural holes; the weak ties that cross them span the holes.

Hundreds of studies in the 1980s and 1990s used email data to ask whether network position predicts success: investment banking, executive recruiting, real estate, engineering teams, factories. They compared two people:

PositionWhat it isWhen it predicted success
CentralityMany ties to people who are tied to each other, at the centre of a tight clusterThe Medici business network in Florence. Before courts, contracts and insurance, social capital was the source of trust: whether you got your money back depended on whom you could trust.
BrokerageConnects otherwise disconnected parts, spanning structural holesModern, largely American and European firms: promotions, higher pay, more commissions. Institutions supply trust now, so the broker's access control and fresh information win.

The readings give metrics for this, such as betweenness centrality. The concept matters more than the formula. Abishna asked about personality: he didn't remember a finding, but a brokerage position only pays if you're comfortable acting as the gatekeeper.

Afternoon — community detection
🗺️
21 · Community Detection
No natural number of pieces
▼

So far the properties belonged to a node (broker, inside a cluster, degree 5) or an edge (bridge, local bridge). Global properties describe the whole graph, and the most common one is community structure.

  • An organisational network (edges = at least a set amount of email between two people, Charles asked). You can eyeball units (maybe accounting, maybe operations), but the same graph can be drawn many ways, so you need an algorithm.
  • The karate club had a clear question: what if it splits in two? Usually you don't know the number. Global airline network (cities connected by direct flights) during COVID: banning travel from one country is pointless if another country in the same cluster stays open.
  • A 14,000-node graph splits trivially into 14,000 one-node pieces, which tells you nothing. There's no logical place to stop, so these methods let you go back and forth depending on your question.
  • A library in Python or R does it for you (it did long before Claude). Know what it's doing: the result is probabilistic, not deterministic.
🛤️
22 · Shortest Paths
Breadth-first search on the early Internet
▼

The example graph was the early Internet: SRI, UCSB, UCLA, Utah, SDC, RAND, Stanford, MIT, BBN, Harvard, Carnegie, Lincoln.

  • Path length = number of edges. Neighbours are at length 1. Between two nodes there may be more than one shortest path: MIT → Carnegie has two paths of length 3.
  • Brute force (list every path for every pair, roughly n(n−1) pairs) is expensive.

Breadth-first search (BFS), from one start node:

  1. Mark the start node's neighbours 1.
  2. Mark the unmarked neighbours of the 1s as 2.
  3. Keep going (3, 4, …) until every node is marked.

Each number is that node's shortest-path length from the start, and following the marks back gives the path. Run it once per node for the whole graph. From UCSB, as heard: two nodes at 1, three at 2, three at 3, two at 4, two at 5.

Check

He called the average slightly more than three. The counts as the recording caught them average 35/12 ≈ 2.9, so one count was probably misheard.

✂️
23 · Edge Removal
Cut the edges on the most shortest paths
▼

A 14-node practice graph. Shortest paths called out in class: 7 → 8 is one hop; 3 → 12 goes 3-7-8-12; 4 → 9 goes 4-6-7-8-9. One edge kept showing up: 7–8.

  1. Find shortest paths and count how many each edge sits on.
  2. Remove the edge on the most shortest paths (7–8 first, which reveals two communities).
  3. Repeat. Here the next ones were 8–12, 8–9, 3–7 and 7–6.
  • Intuition: an edge on many shortest paths connects distant parts, so it's "bridgey". Removing those separates the communities.
  • It's a heuristic, not perfect, but it was a big step forward from pure math and is widely used.
  • On the karate club it finds four well-defined communities, not two. We looked for two only because of the story.
  • There's a stopping rule he didn't cover. Keep removing and every node ends up alone; each algorithm balances how much the next removal breaks the network.
🚶
24 · Random Walks
Walktrap: the default in the library
▼

Random walk: no goal, pick a direction at random. The class voted his steps (forward, left, right, back). In 3D you have six choices, fewer against a wall. Finance models a stock price as a kind of random walk.

On a graph your choices are your neighbours. Node 6 has neighbours 4, 5 and 7, so you flip a three-sided coin.

  • Walk long enough and some edges are traversed far more often. Those are the bridgey edges again, so a random walk is a cheap approximation of shortest-path counting on a big graph.
  • Walktrap (he spelled it out; the transcript caught "walktrab") is what the Python community-detection library implements. Do the random walk and record every jump. Then choose the split into communities where jumps within communities are as large a share as possible and jumps between them as small as possible. That gap also tells you when to stop.
  • Eugene asked whether that assumes the communities up front. No: walk first, then score possible divisions against the recorded jumps.
  • In practice: run the walk around 100 times and average, and add a small chance of jumping to a random part of the graph so the walk doesn't get trapped (PageRank uses the same trick).
Why Walktrap is the standard

Not because it's the most intuitive (removing bridgey edges is). It's standard because, tried on real problems, it gives communities people find useful.

Search and PageRank
🌐
26 · Search
Spider, index, rank
▼

For centuries information was organised in sequence: books, newspapers, an index at the back. Hypertext, the idea the web was built on, organised it as a network, so you could click through the moment you got curious. A student mentioned Obsidian's graph view of linked notes; he said making that useful is genuinely hard.

Most of us now find things by searching (or reading the AI overview), not by surfing. The search engines came in a run: Yahoo's hand-curated directory, then AltaVista (the best of them), InfoSeek, Lycos, Ask Jeeves, Excite, then Google.

JobWhat it means
1 · SpiderFind every page and know where it is
2 · IndexMap keywords to pages. Until about 2022 only Google, Microsoft and Baidu had indexed the web effectively.
3 · RankOrder the matching pages for a query

The others were as good as Google at spidering and indexing. Google changed ranking. Before it, ranking used page content, which was easy to game: paste "Bill Clinton" 500 times in white text at the bottom of your page.

⭐
27 · PageRank
Two definitions, one number
▼

The web is a graph: nodes are pages, and a hyperlink from A to B is a directed edge.

DefinitionIdea
Random surferOver a very long random walk, the share of time spent on each page. In a walled city, a random walker passes through the centre more often than the corners.
Recursive importanceAn important page is one that other important pages point to.

Both definitions give exactly the same numbers. That's compelling, and on top of that it works: Google's "I'm Feeling Lucky" skipped the results list and took you straight to the top page. Using Google for the first time is one of his top three technology experiences.

  • Hubs and authorities (Jon Kleinberg, who wrote our textbook): two scores per page, how authoritative it is and how good it is at pointing to authoritative pages. In PageRank those are one number. It was computationally hard.
  • He said Google combined it with PageRank into something called Hilltop, then went quiet around 2002 as search ads made ranking a trade secret. OpenAI has followed the same open-then-closed path.
  • A page that's never shown as a search result can still have a high PageRank; it just isn't served.
🧮
28 · PageRank in Practice
Always add it as a feature
▼

His own studies: which customers to give a limited number of free products; whether an event around one product moves its neighbours' demand; whether price changes in the network foreshadow demand changes. In every network study he's done, PageRank added value. It's the only network measure that always did.

  • If you've built a network, compute PageRank and add it, as a feature for prediction or a variable for explanation. Your AI system won't add it unless you tell it to.
  • What it captures: the share of the network's attention a node gets.
  • It's global. You can't compute it from one piece of the network; every node's PageRank depends on the whole graph. The day's arc: local node properties → community structure → PageRank, which puts the whole structure into each node.
Our pre-module Amazon graph

The maximum out-degree was 5 because Amazon showed five co-purchased items. The in-degree distribution was very skewed: most nodes have few pointing at them, a few have many.

Degree distributions and power laws
📊
29 · Degree Distributions
The same straight line, everywhere
▼

Summarising a network needs a different baseline from other data. A degree distribution counts how many nodes have each degree.

  • The web's in- and out-degree in 1999, on log-log axes: a downward-sloping straight line. Each 10× increase in degree cuts the frequency by some factor of 10. After 1999 the web got too big, and pages became dynamic, so people stopped measuring it.
  • The same shape shows up in scientific citations, collaboration among rap musicians, athletes and teams, an early Facebook predecessor, Facebook in 2009, and protein interactions in yeast.
The power law

f(k) = a · k−c. Take logs: log f(k) = log a − c · log k, a straight line with slope −c on log-log axes.

Compare heights in a class: most people near the average, a few at each extreme (normal). Networks instead have a huge number of nodes with small degree and a few with degrees millions of times larger.

🎲
30 · Where Power Laws Come From
Dice vs the rich get richer
▼

Why normal: roll one fair die and every face comes up a sixth of the time. Two dice: 7 is most likely (six ways). Three dice: 10 and 11. Four dice: 14. By five dice the shape looks normal. That's the central limit theorem (CLT): an outcome built from many independent random pieces is normally distributed. Height is shaped by genetics, nutrition, sport and more, so heights are normal.

Thought experiment: each new web page arrives with 1–10 links and points them at random existing pages. Then degree would be normal. It isn't.

Now attach slightly preferentially: you're a bit more likely to link to the New York Times, which many pages already link to, than to his page. That alone gives a power law, the way the CLT gives a normal. The rich get richer. Albert-László Barabási (Northeastern) showed this; the recording heard "Garibasi", and the Ugander et al. reading cites Barabási and Albert (1999).

⚠️
31 · Summary Statistics
Plot the data before you trust the mean
▼

Thought experiment: NYU advertises an average MSBA starting salary of $600K. After you've paid, you learn 90% of graduates got no job and 10% started at $6M. Nobody lied; you assumed a normal distribution around the mean.

  • For a power law the variance is undefined (infinite), and the mean doesn't tell you what's typical. An average out-degree of about three says little.
  • So always plot your data and look at the shape. Ask: is it a power law, and what's the exponent? How fast does the tail fall, and how far does it reach?

Questions from the room

  • Human friendship networks rarely follow a power law: constraints cut the tail (nobody has 10 million friends). Follower counts come closer.
  • The airline network doesn't either: one airport can't take 10,000 flights.
  • Word frequencies follow a power law. Wealth does at the high end; at the low end it may be log-normal.
  • You need preferential attachment with nothing capping the number of connections. Hyperlinks have no limit; protein networks are small enough not to hit theirs.
  • Digital spread has no physical friction, but limited attention adds virtual friction: "I only retweet three things a day."

32 · Contagion
Most spread is tiny cascades
▼

Epidemiology built our first model of contagion. People sit in a multilayer contact network: within a neighbourhood, between areas, between cities by air. The question: does a pathogen die out or take over the whole network? There's a phase transition at R0 (R-naught, the average number of people each infected person infects) = 1. At 0.99 it stays local; at 1.01 you have an epidemic. Lockdowns and vaccination push that number down by cutting edges or removing nodes.

Social contagion is different. Companies want the epidemic. But on early Twitter most information spread in tiny cascades: one person to one other, or one to two. Lots of people get news directly rather than from a patient zero. If you only study what goes viral, you miss most of the spread.

The real question: is it contagion at all?

If he told the class that buying a certain book guarantees an A, nearly all of us (connected on LinkedIn) would buy it within a week. It would look viral. It had nothing to do with the network: we were all correlated by sitting in his classroom.

33 · Three Explanations
Influence, selection, or a shared cause
▼

Connected people behaving alike has three possible explanations (the recording picks up after the break, partway through this):

ExplanationDirection
InfluenceConnection → behaviour
SelectionBehaviour → connection: similar people seek each other out
Shared causeSomething else drives both, e.g. growing up somewhere with strong anti-smoking norms

High-school grades show assortative mixing: good students befriend good students. Selection: parents steer you, or advanced classes seat you together. Influence: if your friends study all the time you feel the pressure, and if they look down on studying your grades slip. Shared cause: resources (neighbourhood, activities, a stable home, tutors) shape both whom you meet and your grades.

Why it matters

In network data all three look identical. Parents keeping kids away from smokers, or schools mixing high and low performers, are wasted (even harmful) if there's no real influence. Companies spending to "go viral" when the pattern is really shared demographics should target those characteristics instead. It's harder than correlation vs causation because selection adds another layer.

34 · Social Advertising Experiment
Facebook, Tough Mudder, and the Z × T grid
▼

One of Facebook's earliest social ads: an ad for a page (Tough Mudder) showing names of your friends who'd liked it. Question: does showing friends' names make you more likely to like the page?

  • Z = how many of your friends liked the page (affiliated peers). Buckets 0, 1, 2, 3, 4+. Drop 0 and 4+; keep 1, 2 and 3.
  • T = how many friends' names the ad showed (1, 2 or 3). T can't be bigger than Z; it can be smaller.
  • About ten minutes on a handout. He joked it was 20% of the grade; we kept our sheets.
ComparisonResultWhat it means
Z = 2: shown 2 names vs 1A little over 30% higher responseThe social effect of the ad itself (influence)
Shown 1 name: Z = 2 vs Z = 1About 20% higherNot the ad. Homophily (you share traits with more people who like it), or influence from outside the ad (you've heard about it more).
Why bucket by Z

Just showing some people two names and others one, across everyone, mixes in the Z effect. Comparing within the same Z isolates the effect of showing names. Facebook didn't care why the Z groups differ. He'll post the paper on Brightspace.

35 · Experiments
The control group is an approximation
▼

He said this part won't be tested in the post-module assignment, but be aware: even when you can experiment, network data needs careful design.

  • Randomized controlled trial (RCT): randomly assign a treatment group and a control group.
  • What you really want is the counterfactual: what would have happened to the treated group without the treatment. You can't observe it. The control group's outcome is your approximation, the best one we know.
  • The trap: treating RCTs as the gold standard and choosing business problems by what you can experiment on. That's backwards. The business problem leads; if you can't experiment, do the best you can with observational data.
  • Economists mostly can't experiment (you can't raise rates for half the economy), which is why they built econometrics. In business, interesting things usually happen first and you work out why afterwards.

36 · Propensity Scores
Match on the likelihood of being treated
▼

Observational data: some people got a medicine, some didn't. Covariates may drive both who got it and how they did. Richer patients go to better hospitals; younger people may be likelier to opt in and also recover faster.

  1. Model the probability of being treated from the covariates (age, past health, everything you have). Ignore the outcome at this step. This is separate from instrumental variables.
  2. Score everyone with that model: their propensity score.
  3. Match each treated person with an untreated person who has the same score, and compare outcomes.

Why: with a crystal ball you'd find each person's exact twin, but you can't, and you'd be stuck arguing whether age or income matters more. The score collapses all the covariates into one number. He cited a well-known statistics paper showing this is the best observational approximation of the counterfactual short of an experiment, with some residual issues.

Reference · Day 6
Exam hooks — Day 6
▼
  • Prism vs pipes: structure tells you what things are; flow carries attention, contagion and influence.
  • Karate club: 34 members, 78 friendships. The min cut predicted the split for everyone but node 9 (three weeks from a black belt).
  • Tie strength: the edge is the data point. Comments, messages, wall posts and photo tags predict close friends; likes don't. Significant at about 2× the standard error. Coefficient size depends on the variable's scale. False negatives outnumbered false positives.
  • Bias: selection, volunteer, exclusion, survivorship (Wald's planes), Hawthorne. Network sampling: mixed tie types, missing edges, random samples have no edges → snowball sampling.
  • Thresholds: the ROC curve is the model; the operating point is a decision set by the cost of false positives vs false negatives (COVID US vs China; VCs regret the passes).
  • Granovetter: strong triadic closure + every node has ≥2 strong ties ⇒ every local bridge is a weak tie. Local bridge = endpoints share no neighbour. Weak ties bring new information (jobs).
  • Position: brokerage across structural holes predicted success in modern firms; centrality did in Medici Florence, when trust came from the network.
  • Community detection: no natural number of communities. Remove the edges on the most shortest paths (karate → four). Walktrap = random walk, then maximise within- vs between-community jumps.
  • BFS: mark neighbours 1, theirs 2, and so on; the mark is the shortest-path length.
  • PageRank: random-surfer share = important pages pointed to by important pages. It's global. Always add it as a feature.
  • Power law: f(k) = a·k−c, a straight line on log-log axes, from preferential attachment (the CLT gives normal). Mean and variance mislead, so plot the data.
  • Contagion: R0 = 1 is the phase transition. Social spread is mostly tiny cascades. Influence, selection and a shared cause all look like assortative mixing.
  • Tough Mudder: compare within the same Z to isolate the effect of T (names shown).
  • Propensity score: model P(treated | covariates), then match treated and untreated on the score.
