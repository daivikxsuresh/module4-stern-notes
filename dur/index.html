02 · Framing
Prescriptive analytics and AI — AI is not decision-making
▼

The INFORMS (Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences) definition of business analytics has three layers:

LayerQuestion it answers
DescriptiveWhat happened: historical data, patterns, reporting, visualization
PredictiveWhat will happen: regression through to machine learning
PrescriptiveWhat should we do: optimal decisions that balance every constraint. This course.

Vulcano's position, 2026

  • AI ≠ decision-making. An LLM can generate insights and recommendations but cannot guarantee optimality or feasibility under constraints unless domain knowledge spells those constraints out.
  • Prescriptive analytics needs structure. Optimization turns predictions into actions; simulation measures risk, variability and robustness, which AI output usually ignores.
  • Transparency. Optimization and simulation give explainable, auditable results, which is what lets you validate or challenge an AI recommendation.
  • Synergy. Humans design the model; AI automates data and scenario exploration. Use AI for work you could do yourself but that is tedious.
Risk 1
Outsourcing judgment. Leaning on AI erodes domain knowledge and ownership of the decision.
Risk 2
The black box. It is hard to pull the structural reasoning out of an AI answer, or to audit its logic.

The class debate

Prompting is the bottleneck: getting every constraint and all the context into a prompt is genuinely hard. A student argued that agentic AI (Claude with connectors, which tolerates typos and missing context) is the near-term workaround. Vulcano admitted he was unsure where that goes but stayed cautious: domain knowledge is the anchor. A second thread covered training data. LLMs learn from biased or incomplete corpora, and history is interpretation, not exact science. The answer you get depends on which interpretation was in the training set.

03 · The Map
Decision complexity vs. uncertainty complexity
▼

Every model in the course sits on two axes: how many decisions you are choosing between, and how richly you describe the uncertainty.

QuadrantStrengthSoftware
Deterministic optimizationExplores many decision alternatives, but shrinks uncertainty to fixed numbersExcel Solver + AI-generated scripts
Decision treesFew decisions, few discrete outcomesTreePlan
SimulationRich uncertainty, but explores only a handful of decisionsCrystal Ball + AI-generated scripts
Stochastic optimizationBoth at once. The most powerful and the most expensive to computeAI-generated scripts

The decision modeling process

  1. Define the real-world managerial problem
  2. Gather and analyze data
  3. Formulate a model of the problem
  4. Derive solutions
  5. Test the model and refine. Did it include every relevant factor? Are the solutions reasonable? Does it beat what was done before?
  6. Apply the model and make recommendations
  7. Implement

04 · Decision Trees
Three nodes, three ingredients, one number (EMV)
▼

A decision tree is a picture of a decision under uncertainty. It is not the machine-learning decision tree from Module 3; it only shares the name.

NodeShapeMeaning
DecisionSquareA choice you control
Event (chance)CircleA random outcome you don't control, with probabilities
Terminal (leaf)End of branchThe payoff collected at the end of that path

Three ingredients every decision model needs

  1. The decisions: the alternatives
  2. The outcomes and their probabilities
  3. A value model linking decisions and outcomes to payoffs

Solving it — fold back

Add cash flows up going forward along each path, then roll back right to left. At a chance node, take the EMV (Expected Monetary Value), the probability-weighted average of its branches. At a decision node, keep the best branch. A policy is a decision for each piece of information you could have when you decide.

  • Single-stage: one decision. Multistage: decide → observe → collect → decide again, laid out as a tree.
  • Uses: bidding for contracts, product launches, capacity planning, personal calls like buying a house.
When EMV is the wrong yardstick

Maximizing EMV assumes risk neutrality. That's fine when this decision is one of many in a portfolio, because the averages wash out. It is not fine for a one-off, high-stakes decision. That is what utility theory (card 09) is for.

TreePlan habits

Park every probability in a named cell (e.g. p1) and point every branch at it. In class, moving p1 from 0.3 to 0.4 flipped the optimal alternative. That's the robustness check. Copy subtrees through TreePlan, copy the whole sheet to duplicate a tree, and never insert or delete rows inside the diagram.

05 · Worked Example
Magnolia Inn — buy land before the airport is announced
▼

An airport is going to site A (probability 0.4) or site B (0.6). Land next to the winning site is worth a lot; land next to the loser has to be resold. Amounts in $ millions:

OptionCostIf AIf BNet (A, B)EMV
Buy A1831resale 6(13, −12)−2.0
Buy B12resale 423(−8, 11)3.4 ✓
Buy both30(5, −1)1.4
Buy neither0(0, 0)0

Decision: buy B. EMV = 0.4 × (−8) + 0.6 × 11 = 3.4.

With perfect information you would buy whichever site wins: 0.4 × 13 + 0.6 × 11 = 11.8. So EVPI = 11.8 − 3.4 = 8.4 (card 06).

06 · Value of Information
EVPI — the clairvoyant tree
▼

EVPI (Expected Value of Perfect Information) = EMV with perfect information − EMV of the baseline tree. It is the ceiling on what any information is worth, because no study can beat a clairvoyant.

Building the clairvoyant tree

  1. Start the tree with an event node: the envelope. The clairvoyant says hit or flop, with the prior probabilities.
  2. Once the envelope is open, the downstream probabilities become 0 or 1.
  3. Then the decision node: you choose knowing the answer.
  4. You pay the clairvoyant before you know what she'll say, so EVPI is an expected value.

High School Musical 4 (HSM4)

Prior: hit 0.4, flop 0.6. Release in theaters (25, −20), release on the channel (8, −2), or cancel (0, 0), in $ millions.

TreeBest playEMV
BaselineChannel: 0.4 × 8 + 0.6 × (−2)2
Perfect infoTheaters if hit, cancel if flop: 0.4 × 2510
EVPI10 − 28
Reading it

A big EVPI says go and look for information. A small one says don't bother: even perfect information wouldn't pay. Compute EVPI first, before pricing any real study.

07 · Value of Information
Imperfect information — flip the probabilities with Bayes
▼

Real information is a market research study that comes back "encouraging" or "discouraging". Its value is the EVSI (Expected Value of Sample Information), also called the value of imperfect information:

EVSI = EMV with sample info − EMV without info

The trap — time order

The study's track record comes as P(result | hit or flop), which is backwards in time. The tree needs P(hit or flop | result), because you see the result first. Bayes' rule does the flip.

The Bayes table (HSM4)

P(encouraging | hit) = 0.6 · P(encouraging | flop) = 0.3.

StepEncouragingDiscouraging
Joint · hit0.4 × 0.6 = 0.240.4 × 0.4 = 0.16
Joint · flop0.6 × 0.3 = 0.180.6 × 0.7 = 0.42
Marginal0.420.58
P(hit | result)0.24 / 0.42 = 4/70.16 / 0.58 = 8/29

Joints first, marginals by adding the joints, posteriors by dividing. The marginals go on the first event node (the study result); the posteriors go on the hit/flop nodes after the decision.

The tree and the answer

  • Encouraging → theaters (EMV 5.71) · Discouraging → channel (EMV 0.76)
  • EMV with the study = 0.42 × 5.71 + 0.58 × 0.76 = 2.84
  • EVSI = 2.84 − 2 = 0.84, so the most you'd pay for the study is $840,000
The rule

Information value is never negative, because you can always ignore it. It is positive only if it changes at least one decision. Here it does: theaters on good news, channel on bad. The Bayes spreadsheet is on Brightspace.

08 · Two-Stage Trees
Test market first, then decide — Morton Ward
▼

Same logic, one more decision: test or not, then introduce or not after seeing the result. Morton Ward (problem set p. 4): success/failure prior 0.5/0.5; launch pays (40, −15), no launch 0; the test costs 2 ($ millions); P(approved | success) = 0.8, P(approved | failure) = 0.25.

TreeWorkingEMV

09 · Utility Theory
Risk aversion, certainty equivalents, risk premiums
▼

Utility theory is a formal way to encode an attitude to risk. Money goes in, utility points come out.

ShapeAttitudeFeels like
Concave, increasingRisk-averseLosses hurt more than equal gains help. Most people, on gains.
Straight lineRisk-neutralGains and losses symmetric. This is EMV.
ConvexRisk-seekingGains valued more than losses

The steeper the curve bends, the more risk-averse. On the slide's concave curve, +$30K is worth +0.25 utility points but −$30K costs −0.5. Consumers are typically risk-averse on gains and risk-seeking on losses (Kahneman and Tversky, Prospect Theory, 1979).

The fundamental property

You are indifferent between two alternatives with the same expected utility. Example: a lottery paying $100,000 with probability 0.25 (else 0) has expected utility 0.25, the same as $10,000 for sure. So you're indifferent between them.

Two definitions to know cold

Certainty Equivalent (CE): the sure amount that makes you indifferent to the gamble. For a risk-averse person CE < EMV.
Risk premium = EMV − CE: what you'd give up to avoid the gamble.

The recipe

  1. Turn every payoff into utility points with U(x)
  2. Take the probability-weighted average: the expected utility
  3. Turn it back into dollars with the inverse function: that's the CE
  4. Choose the alternative with the highest expected utility, which is the same as the highest CE

Deal or No Deal is the illustration. The contestant is offered $341K; the EMV of playing on is higher. A risk-neutral player refuses, but a risk-averse player can rationally take the deal. Utility makes that formal (card 11).

10 · Worked Example
Log utility — should you enter the competition?
▼

You have $10,000. Entry costs $3,000 and gives a 1/3 chance of winning $19,000. U(x) = ln x, where x is final wealth, not the incremental payoff.

QuantityWorkingValue
Final wealthWin: 10,000 − 3,000 + 19,000 · Lose: 10,000 − 3,00026,000 / 7,000
EMV⅓ × 26,000 + ⅔ × 7,000$13,333
Expected utility⅓ ln 26,000 + ⅔ ln 7,0009.2911
CEe9.2911$10,841
Risk premium13,333 − 10,841$2,493

Enter. The CE ($10,841) beats the $10,000 you hold for sure; equivalently 9.2911 > ln 10,000 = 9.2103.

Trap

Log utility only works on positive total wealth. Feed it the incremental payoff (−3,000) and it breaks.

11 · Utility in TreePlan
Exponential utility and the risk tolerance RT
▼

U(x) = A − B·e−x/RT

This is TreePlan's function. Its advantage is one parameter to tune: RT, the risk tolerance, in the same money units as the payoffs. Set A, B and RT in named cells.

  • Smaller RT → more risk-averse. Larger RT → closer to risk-neutral.
  • RT → ∞ gives back EMV maximization.
  • CE = −RT · ln E[e−X/RT]

Magnolia with RT = 100 ($M), A = B = 1

Buying B has expected utility 0.029185 and CE = 2.96, below its EMV of 3.4. The 0.44 gap is the risk premium.

Calibrating RT — Deal or No Deal

A 50/50 lottery of $1,000,000 or $25 (EMV $500,012.50) against a sure $341,000.

  1. Build the tree in TreePlan with exponential utility on.
  2. Goal Seek (Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek): set the lottery's CE equal to 341,000 by changing RT.
  3. That gives the threshold RT ≈ $731,548. Below it, take the deal; above it, play.

Check: at RT = $500,000 the lottery's CE is ≈ $283,132, less than $341,000, so a player that risk-averse takes the deal.

In practice

Investment questionnaires infer your RT the same way, from a sequence of hypothetical lotteries. Organizations have different RTs at different levels; lower-level managers tend to be more risk-averse than the firm as a whole.

Don't reuse slide 19's number

Encoding Risk p. 10 (slide 19) gives RT = 338,300 for indifference between paying $10K and facing a 10% chance of a $100K loss. The $10K premium equals the expected loss, so a strictly risk-averse decision-maker always prefers to pay. No finite RT makes them indifferent.

12 · LP Refresher
Wyndor Glass — formulate, graph, solve
▼

LP (linear programming) allocates scarce resources among competing activities. Everything must be linear. It is still the most-used optimization tool in practice because it solves fast and bends to most problems.

Assumptions

  • Linearity: no fixed costs, no economies of scale
  • Certainty: every parameter is known
  • Divisibility: fractional answers are allowed

The model

Doors x1, windows x2 per week. Max 300x1 + 500x2 subject to:

ConstraintHours/weekAt optimum
Plant 1 · x1 ≤ 404020 used — slack 20
Plant 2 · 2x2 ≤ 120120binding
Plant 3 · 3x1 + 2x2 ≤ 180180binding

Optimum: 20 doors, 60 windows, $36,000.

Graphically (two variables)

Each constraint is a half-plane; the feasible region is where they all overlap. Push the objective line in the improving direction until it last touches the region. The optimum is always at a corner point.

Excel Solver

  1. Objective = profit cell; changing cells = x1, x2; add constraints LHS ≤ RHS
  2. Tick "Make unconstrained variables non-negative"; engine = Simplex LP
  3. Ctrl+click to generate both the Answer Report and the Sensitivity Report

13 · Sensitivity
Shadow prices, allowable ranges, reduced costs
▼

A shadow price is how much the objective improves per one extra unit of a resource (the constraint's RHS, right-hand side), with everything else held fixed.

ConstraintStatusShadow price
Plant 1Slack 20$0, spare hours are worth nothing
Plant 2Binding$150/hour, valid for RHS 60 to 180
Plant 3Binding$100/hour
  • The price only holds within the allowable increase/decrease. Beyond it, re-solve. Diminishing returns are guaranteed: past the range the shadow price can stay or fall, never rise.
  • +50 Plant 2 hours: 50 ≤ 60 allowable, so profit rises 50 × $150 = $7,500 to $43,500.
  • The shadow price tells you the new profit, not the new production plan. For the plan, re-solve.

Should we add a product? — coffee tables

Each table uses 1 hour of Plant 2 and 2 hours of Plant 3. Opportunity cost = 1 × $150 + 2 × $100 = $350. It earns $250, so the reduced cost is −$100: don't make it unless its profit rises by at least $100.

Pathological case

A non-binding constraint always has a shadow price of 0, but the converse isn't guaranteed. A binding constraint can also show 0 if it's redundant.

💯
14 · Sensitivity
The 100% rule and objective-coefficient ranging
▼

Changing several RHS values at once: express each change as a % of its allowable increase or decrease (by direction), take absolute values, add them up.

  • Sum ≤ 100% → the shadow prices still hold
  • Sum > 100% → inconclusive, so re-solve
Change% of allowableProfit effect
Plant 2 +30 hrs30 / 60 = 50%30 × $150 = $4,500
Plant 3 +20 hrs20 / 60 = 33%20 × $100 = $2,000
Total83% ≤ 100% ✓+$6,500 → $42,500, confirmed by re-solving

Objective-coefficient ranging

The other half of the Sensitivity Report. It's the allowable increase/decrease on each profit coefficient within which the optimal mix doesn't change. Total profit still moves; the mix doesn't. Door profit can rise from $300 to $750 before (20, 60) stops being optimal. The 100% rule applies here too, for simultaneous coefficient changes.

Keep the two apart

RHS ranges → is the shadow price still valid? Coefficient ranges → is the production mix still optimal?

15 · AI-Assisted Modeling
Pyomo + GLPK with Claude — prompt, audit, run
▼

Pyomo is a Python modeling language; GLPK (the GNU Linear Programming Kit) is the solver engine. Pyomo sits between Python and GLPK so the model reads like the math.

Workflow

  1. Prompt Claude with a structured model: variables with units and ≥ 0, the objective, one line per named constraint, and the output labels you want
  2. Audit the script before you run it
  3. Run it in Spyder (preferred: Pyomo and GLPK stay installed offline) or Google Colab (reinstall both every session)
  4. Check the output against Excel Solver. Wyndor must come back 20 / 60 / $36,000

What to find in the script

PieceLook for
Variablesthe declarations and their domain (non-negative)
Objectivethe maximize/minimize expression
Constraintsnamed: plant_one, plant_two, plant_three
Solvemodel build + GLPK call
Results.value() on variables and the objective
Dualsmodel.dual → shadow prices · model.rc → reduced costs (declared as an import Suffix before solving)
What Pyomo won't give you

Allowable ranges. Excel's Sensitivity Report has them; Pyomo's duals don't. Build them yourself with re-solves (card 16).

Spot the bug

Wyndor_Spot_the_Bug.py on Brightspace runs cleanly and returns $30,000 instead of $36,000. The groups found two modeling errors: Plant 2 written as ≥ 120, and Plant 3's coefficients swapped (2x1 + 3x2 ≤ 180). Its answer was (0, 60).

The lesson

A script that runs without error isn't a validated model. You need to understand the model's structure to audit AI-generated code, even if you couldn't have written it from scratch.

The allowable increase doesn't forbid buying a second block. It only says the $20 shadow price isn't guaranteed past +10M. A complete answer re-solves for the second block, which turns out to be worthless. Beyond the range the shadow price can only stay the same or fall.

Day 1 exam hooks — the twelve things worth memorising
▼
  1. Square = decision, circle = chance, leaf = payoff. Roll back right to left: average at circles, best at squares.
  2. EMV assumes risk neutrality. Fine for a portfolio of decisions, wrong for a one-off big bet.
  3. EVPI = EMV with perfect info − baseline EMV. It's the ceiling on any information. HSM4: 10 − 2 = 8. Magnolia: 11.8 − 3.4 = 8.4.
  4. EVSI = EMV with sample info − baseline EMV. HSM4: 2.84 − 2 = 0.84 → pay at most $840K for the study.
  5. Bayes flips time order: joints → marginals (first chance node) → posteriors (after the decision).
  6. Information is never worth less than zero, and is worth something only if it changes a decision.
  7. Concave = risk-averse; CE < EMV; risk premium = EMV − CE. Log utility uses final wealth.
  8. U = A − B·e−x/RT: smaller RT = more risk-averse; RT → ∞ = EMV. Calibrate RT with Goal Seek (Deal or No Deal ≈ $731,548).
  9. Shadow price = profit per extra unit of RHS, only inside the allowable range. Past it, re-solve; it never goes up.
  10. 100% rule: sum the |change| ÷ allowable; ≤ 100% means the shadow prices still hold. Wyndor: 50% + 33% = 83% → $42,500.
  11. Reduced cost = profit − opportunity cost of the resources used. Coffee table: 250 − (150 + 200) = −100 → don't make it.
  12. Pyomo gives duals and reduced costs, not ranges. Audit the AI script against Excel Solver: the bug script's $30,000 vs the true $36,000.
The one-line summary of Day 1

Every tool today answers the same question: what is this option worth once you price in what you don't know? Trees price uncertainty, information is priced by how many decisions it changes, utility prices your nerve, and shadow prices price a resource, but only locally. When you leave the range, re-solve.
