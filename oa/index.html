03 · Variation
Variation beats volume, and A/B tests are the cleanest way to get it
▼

Two years of sales at one constant price can't show how demand responds to price. A much smaller dataset that covers several prices tells you more. "The variation of the data is sometimes more important than the quantity of the data."

He presented A/B testing as both causal inference (random assignment lets you attribute the difference to the treatment) and decision support. His example: a T-shirt at $49 vs $59, 1,000 visitors in each group, compare once there are enough observations. You test charm pricing ($9.95 vs $10) rather than assume it works.

The two ends of retail data

Point-of-sale data is structured. E-commerce data is richer and messier: clickstream, search keywords, time on page, scrolling. It can be massive and hard to manage. A campus store with about 60 cameras tracked where people walked and how long they stayed, for layout and product placement.

The promotion case: previewed, not taught

Supermarket margins average about 1.7%, so a few points of profit matter at scale. He spent three and a half years on the case. The AI beat managers' promotion decisions because it could process far more data and optimize carefully. He was clear this isn't a claim that AI is always better than people.

Open

Reading he named: Lessons from More Than 1,000 E-Commerce Pricing Tests (on disk in Readings & Cases) and the Intelligems pricing case. Optional: his Columbia Business School talk with co-founder Bruno Boni, Mon Nov 9.

Markdown optimization — the retailer game
🏷️
04 · Markdowns
Why fashion retailers mark down: price skimming
▼

Econ 101 with zero marginal cost: one price p* earns revenue only from customers willing to pay at least p*. Two prices earn more: customers willing to pay phi or higher pay phi, and those between plo and phi buy at plo.

Fashion has the conditions for revenue management: a long lead time (you're stuck with inventory ordered months ahead) and a very mixed population. Trend-driven customers are inelastic and pay full price early; elastic customers care about trend less and have less money. Selling to both over a season means starting high and marking down: price markdowns, a.k.a. price skimming.

The economics he drew

  • With D(p) = a − bp, revenue = p × D(p), the rectangle under the demand curve. Find p* by setting the derivative to zero or by a grid search.
  • The two-price idea comes up via Hal Varian (former Berkeley professor, Google chief economist).
  • Markdowns are time-based price discrimination: different customers pay different prices for the same product at different times. Traditional price discrimination charges different people different prices at the same time. A student asked if this creates deadweight loss: he said yes and didn't elaborate.
  • Lead times of 6–8 months from Asia are why there's no mid-season replenishment.
  • Positioning: Hermès and Dior at the luxury end, H&M and Costco at the price-sensitive end. Zara starts high and marks down, serving both early buyers and people willing to wait.
  • Markdowns ≠ dynamic pricing. Uber's price changes continually with the market; fashion sets a price and only cuts it. Electronic shelf labels allow more frequent changes.

07 · Demand Estimation
Lift factors: absolute demand varies, the relative lift doesn't
▼

Every run is a different item with its own demand curve, and demand is random week to week even at one price. The hint: the lift from a price drop is similar across items. If $60 → $54 lifts one item's sales 25%, it lifts others about 25% too.

From Sales-Data.xlsx: average weekly sales at each price per item, ignoring stock-out weeks, then the ratio to the $60 weeks.

Real-time data pins down the item. Sell 59 at $60 in week 1, and assume 59/week at $60, 59 × 1.31 = 77 at $54, 59 × 1.73 = 102 at $48, 59 × 2.81 = 166 at $36.

PriceUnits/weekRevenue/weekStep up from the price before

Lots of time to sell 2,000 units → $60; very little time → $36. A long horizon lets you hold a high price; one week left forces it.

What he warned about

  • Everything rests on the 59. If it's noisy, every forecast is noisy. A second observation of 61 builds confidence; an 88 says you need more data.
  • 15 runs may be too few. Replay or scrape the game for more; he mentioned 10,000 runs as an example, not a requirement.
  • Similar lift across items is an assumption to test, not a rule. Socks, winter jackets and T-shirts may need separate factors: cluster by business judgment or machine learning, one lift per cluster.
  • A new product's first price is close to a guess. Use comparable products and earlier seasons. A $60 item needing two cuts right after week 1 means the estimate was badly off.

09 · LP Heuristic
The LP: weeks at each price, under time and inventory
▼

Decision variables: x60, x54, x48, x36 = number of weeks sold at each price.

PartFormula

Excel Solver's answer: weeks 2–7 at 20% off ($48), weeks 8–15 at 40% off ($36). Revenue $3,540 + $77,182 = $80,772. Same plan as the run-out rate heuristic. The LP "understood that it was optimal to sell the last T-shirt on the last day."

  • Garbage in: inaccurate demand estimates make the LP solve the wrong problem. Check whether the answer makes business sense.
  • Fractional weeks may need handling in a real schedule; this example came out in whole weeks.
  • $80,772 vs $82,404: the recording summary leaves this unexplained. My read: $80,772 is the plan's expected revenue at week 1, using 59/week, and $82,404 is what one game actually paid under the same schedule.
Doing better: re-optimization

Here the LP is close to the best possible, but you can often beat it: re-solve every week with the updated demand estimate (one more week of sales) and the actual remaining inventory. Extensions: richer demand models, multiple items (cannibalization), limits on the number of markdowns, choosing the price points themselves. Zara is the practice example.

10 · Robustness
Re-optimize, stress-test, then report a distribution
▼

Re-optimization

Update demand and inventory after a week's sales, then re-solve for weeks 3–15. Weekly is one option; he said companies often update every two or four weeks because refreshing data and solving costs money. You balance that cost against fresher information. For the assignment extension he suggested every 4–5 weeks.

Robustness

Change the inputs (59 vs 50, or forecasts ±10%) and re-solve. "If I find a drastically different solution, it doesn't provide confidence that my solution is robust."

How to validate

  1. Is the solution intuitive, and does it make business sense?
  2. Run many cases, not one: 1,000–10,000 game instances came up.
  3. Report the average, standard deviation, confidence interval, and the best and worst parts of the distribution. A target of within 0.5% of optimal on average came up; the calculation wasn't specified.
  4. Test against noisy or misspecified forecasts before implementing.

At scale: 50,000–80,000 Zara products × 10 perturbations each ≈ a million LPs, so you pick which inputs to perturb, and backtesting helps choose.

Extensions he listed

Richer demand models · multiple items and cannibalization (a blue T-shirt markdown pulls from the green one) · size mix (one example: 40% M, 20% S, 10% XS, 20% L, 10% XL) · limits such as a cap on markdowns or 2–3 weeks between them · optimizing the price points themselves.

The weekly rule

12 · Inventory
Why hold inventory, and why it's called evil
▼
Reasons to hold itReasons not to
Demand, supply and process uncertaintyHolding cost, insurance
Quantity discounts, ordering/setup costDecisions made in advance; limited space
Price swings and inflation; peak demand comingRisk that preferences change; quality deteriorates
Protection against stock-outs

Inventory is a huge commitment: about 30–60% of total assets in many retailers. It touches every part of daily operations, and managing it well is a real edge (Apple, Zara, Walmart).

  • Apple: Tim Cook nearly eliminated stockpiled inventory and called it "fundamentally evil". Stock sitting in a warehouse costs money instead of making it.
  • Zara: most apparel retailers lock in 40–60% of a season's line six months ahead and ~80% by season start. Zara commits only 15–25% six months out and 50–60% by season start, designing and making the rest mid-season.
  • Walmart: turns its inventory about 8 times a year (Target 6.4). Turn faster than your 30-day payment terms and your vendors finance your inventory, which can mean lower costs, lower prices and more sales. He cautioned that it takes buying power and scale.

13 · Newsvendor
Marginal analysis: the pumpkin example
▼

The newsvendor buys papers each morning: too few loses sales, too many leaves unsold copies. The problem has three traits: uncertain demand, a perishable or seasonal product with no replenishment, and an order placed before demand is known. Christmas trees, Halloween pumpkins, skiwear, flu vaccines, seats held back for last-minute bookings.

Pumpkins: P(D ≤ 200) = 0.5, P(D ≤ 250) = 0.75, P(D ≤ 300) = 0.9. Buy at $2, sell at $5, salvage $1.

Extra unitExpected value of that unitBuy it?
201st0.5 × ($1 − $2) + 0.5 × ($5 − $2) = +$1Yes
301st0.9 × ($1 − $2) + 0.1 × ($5 − $2) = −$0.60No
251st0.75 × (−$1) + 0.25 × ($3) = $0Indifferent

Order 250 (or 251). The profit curve rises ("too little"), peaks ("just right"), then falls ("too much").

The tie at 251: Eugene's view was don't order it, since it adds no expected gain and uses space. The other view: it could serve one more customer if demand runs high. Someone asked whether the calculation should include a holding cost; the recording stops before the answer. Where demand is known well ahead, such as a customized car, the model matters less.

Key idea

At the optimal order quantity, the expected profit of ordering one more unit is zero.

14 · Critical Fractile
P(D ≤ X) = cu / (cu + co)
▼
  1. Forecast the distribution: P(D ≤ X) for each X.
  2. Costs: overstocking co = cost of a unit nobody buys; understocking cu = cost of a sale you missed. Pumpkins: co = 2 − 1 = 1, cu = 5 − 2 = 3.
  3. Marginal unit X+1 is worth −co·P(D ≤ X) + cu·P(D > X).
  4. Set it to zero: P(D ≤ X) = cu / (cu + co).

The ratio is the critical fractile, and it is also the service level (the chance you don't run out). Then read X off the demand distribution. For normal demand: X = μ + zσ, with z the standard-normal value at the critical fractile (z-table, or NORMINV(fractile, μ, σ) in Excel).

T-shirts

Demand N(1,000, 200); cost $10, price $15, salvage $8 → cu = 5, co = 2, fractile 5/7 = 0.714, z = 0.57 → 1,114 shirts (NORMINV(5/7, 1000, 200)).

"Price drops to $12?" cu = 2 and co = 2 → fractile 0.5 → z = 0 → order 1,000. That's the balanced-cost case, not a rule that the mean is always right.

The executive trap

In his exercise with executives, more than half ordered average demand, whatever the costs were. Push the price to $1 million and a missed sale is catastrophic, so order far more. At $10.10 you make $0.10 a sale against a $2 overstock, so order far less.

  • Read the fractile as a threshold: how likely must the next unit be to sell, given the two costs.
  • Normal table: cumulative 98.7% maps to z = 2.23 (table entry 0.9871).
  • Service levels: 99%, 99.9% or 99.99% get expensive fast. Uber's targets may vary by time of day, ZIP code and city.
  • Demand may not be normal. With enough data, read X off the empirical or simulated distribution; the normal formula is for when you only have a mean and standard deviation.
Inventory management — Champagne, many periods

15 · Champagne Case
A liquor store's Champagne: hold vs backlog, every week
▼

A liquor store wants a better ordering policy for Champagne. Shelf space is tight (a quarter shelf per brand) and holding bottles is costly (temperature, humidity). Under its loyalty program, a customer whose bottle isn't in stock gets a $3 discount per bottle for each week late, so unmet demand is back-ordered, not lost.

InputValue
Demand per periodNormal, μ = 100, σ = 20 (unknown when you order)
TimingCount and order Sunday evening → shipped overnight → delivered before Monday opening, so zero lead time. You order before seeing the week's demand
Holding cost$1.5 per unit per period
Backlog cost$3 per unit per period
Current policyIf ending inventory ≤ 20, order 100; otherwise don't. Two numbers, safety stock 20

Too much → holding cost; too little → backlog cost. Backlog costs twice holding, so the case says any optimal policy leans toward over-ordering. Objective 1: the average cost per period of the current policy. Objective 2: beat it.

  • Backorder vs lost sale: here the customer puts down a 20% deposit and waits. With milk at a grocery they just leave for a competitor.
  • 100 and 20 aren't random: the case points out that David's two numbers are exactly the mean and the standard deviation. Constant orders suit the supplier and are easy to run.
  • The consulting workflow he described: understand the current operation, simulate its baseline cost, then propose and test better policies.

The current policy is intuitive and simple, but it performs badly. Ordering exactly the mean (100) only when stock is low means it never catches up, so the backlog wanders like a random walk. Ordering a bit more than the mean (115) from a higher trigger (70) fixes the drift. Re-run it with F9: "the cost is totally unpredictable."

Where do 30 and 135 come from?

This came up at the end of the session. Two ways were proposed: formulate it as an optimization problem, or brute-force candidate values and compare. Nothing was decided. The figure is average cost per period, not per bottle and not a total, which is what the "3,000 vs 120 × 3 = 360" confusion was about. Also, the demand mean and standard deviation are inputs; the manager's decisions are only the two policy numbers.

Slide 31: what's wrong with any constant policy

Beginning inventory is often negative. The order size should depend on how much is left. And since demand has the same distribution every period, you'd ideally start every period at the same inventory level.

17 · Optimal Policy
Order-up-to 108.614: the newsvendor, every period
▼

Slide 32 (blank in the students' copy; from my photo):

  • Use the newsvendor formula: understocking = backlog $3, overstocking = holding $1.5 → =NORMINV(3/4.5, 100, 20) = 108.614.
  • Each period, order exactly enough to bring inventory back up to 108.614: =IF(108.614 - D2 > 0, 108.614 - D2, 0).
  • This is the order-up-to policy, and it is optimal. Average cost ≈ $32 (my simulation: $32.64).
Which cell

It's the order quantity, column E. The slide says "E1" but points at D2: in Cohen's sheet row 1 holds the headers, so period 1 is row 2. Put it in E2 and fill down. Column F stays the cost.

Where it's used: hospitals keep an order-up-to level for each blood type and refill daily (shortages vs outdated red cells); Tesco refills high-volume non-perishables up to three times a day; car dealers, electronics.

Summary

Simulation lets you test and compare policies and their robustness (e.g., change the demand distribution, or move the mean from 100 to 110). A robust policy still performs acceptably when the assumptions change. The constant policy is easy but performs badly; order-up-to is optimal and widely used.

    • Spikes around November–December: tech gadgets around the holidays.
    • 2018 prices above 2016: a trend (inflation, or the brand changing).
    • A September spike: maybe back-to-school. Flag it for someone who knows the business.
✂️
20 · Train / Test
Never split a time series at random
▼

A random 70/30 split puts, say, December 2018 in training and November 2018 in testing. December teaches the model things that make November too easy, and in real life you never have December's data before November's. So cut chronologically: everything before a date (around Feb 2018 here) trains, everything after tests. The notebooks take the first 70% of each SKU's 98 weeks (68) as training and the last 30 as test.

Sliding-window cross-validation

The time-series version of k-fold: train on weeks 1–8, test on 9; slide one step and repeat. Always predict forward. Score each window and average.

Window size

At least a year, so every window contains all twelve months. Windows shorter than the seasonality (summer, December) can't learn it, which is a red flag.

Why backtest at all: stakeholder confidence. A merchandiser may place orders for hundreds or thousands of units off this forecast.

🕳️
21 · Missing Data
Assume the data is bad
▼

Color is missing for a few products, which is physically impossible. The options:

  1. Drop the rows: you lose information.
  2. Frequency imputation: fill with the SKU's most common value. Better, but still a guess.
  3. Model-based imputation: train a model to predict color, then forecast on the improved data.
  4. Use a model that handles missing values natively, such as XGBoost.
  5. Keep missingness as a signal: maybe it's a different product type, or a color the site never set.

Missing data is usually not random (a sensor that breaks in harsh conditions is a pattern). Here it clusters on a few SKUs (43, 44, 42, 9; 10 rows, functionality 9 and 11), mostly mid-to-high-price products, with no strong pattern. SKU 43's most common color is gold, so that's what it gets. A human or AI reading the product image could fill color too; Mechanical Turk works only for what's visible, not specialist product knowledge. The notebook fixes it two ways: manual (sales.at[row,'color'] = 'black') and scikit-learn's SimpleImputer(strategy= 'most_frequent') fitted per SKU. With colors there's no average color, only the most frequent one.

How he does it at work

Write down your assumption, put screenshots of the bad data on simple slides, and run a one-hour workshop with the engineers or product experts so they can confirm or refute it. One or two rounds gets you far. Keep the code modular (preprocessing, method 1, method 2 in separate blocks) so you can rerun after learning something.

22 · Outliers
Find them per SKU; think hard before dropping them
▼

An outlier is a statistically abnormal point. The notebook's check_outliers flags values beyond mean ± k·σ within each SKU (groupby('sku').transform). At k = 3 the class saw about 50 of ~4,000 rows (~1%); at k = 5, a handful, including a value of about 1,130. Mean ± kσ suits roughly normal data; for long-tailed data use the interquartile range: [Q1 − 1.5·IQR, Q3 + 1.5·IQR].

Checked

That 1,130 can't be a price: the highest price in data_raw.csv is $227.72. The data does have 1,133 units sold (SKU 25, week of Sep 3 2018, at $6.51). On the raw file, k = 3 flags 32 prices and 85 sales weeks, so I can't tie out the class's ~50.

Dropping is risky

If the "outlier" is the best sales week of the year and you drop it, that is exactly the week your model will miss worst in production, and you'll badly under-order. Check with a domain expert first. Alternatives: regularization (Lasso/Ridge), or models that cope with outliers.

A September spike might be back-to-school, so ask. A high price is normal for a MacBook and strange for an iPhone case, which is why it's per SKU. Explore on raw values, not normalized ones. "$100 against a usual $40 ± 10" is easy to reason about; "0.78" is not.

23 · Features
Trend, month dummies, lagged prices
▼

Trend vs seasonality: the trend is the long-term movement (the sea level rising: inflation, a brand getting cooler); seasonality is the recurring movement (the tide). Paydays and pension days are seasonality too.

  • Trend = year − 2016, so the first week (Oct 31 2016) is 0 and it counts up.
  • Month as dummies, not 1–12. As an integer, December "beats" January, but the year is cyclical. pd.get_dummies(..., drop_first=True) drops January: it's the baseline, and keeping every dummy adds collinear, redundant columns that confuse the model.
  • Lagged prices: groupby('sku')['price'].shift(1) and shift(2) give price-1 and price-2 within the same SKU. That gives the model price history (deal-seeking customers). The first two rows per SKU become NaN and are dropped; losing two rows is cheap.
  • Other candidates: cost of goods, competitor prices (retailers pay for software to monitor them; one competitor move can kill your sales), relative price on the listing page, marketing spend, product launches. Plus feat_main_page → int and dummies for functionality, color and vendor.

Save versioned checkpoints of the processed data and note what you did. Caveat: steps with randomness (e.g. random imputation) can give different results on each rerun unless you fix the seed.

🌱
24 · Sparse + Interactions
One data point, new products, feature counts, interactions
▼
  • Sparse history: one December sale can't teach a month pattern, or how the product sells in other months. A forecast that doesn't vary by month is a limited fallback.
  • New product, no history: borrow from similar products or prior launches (an older MacBook for a new one). Clustering is the advanced way to share information.
  • Variation in features and target: if vendor, functionality and price stay put but sales swing, the model is missing a driver: competitors, weather, the Super Bowl.
  • Feature count: rule of thumb, about one feature per 10 rows, not a hard limit. Plain linear regression can fail with more columns than rows.
  • Interactions capture features working together: your low price matters more when a competitor's is high; ice cream depends on category × season. Combinations explode, so start with a few plausible ones from domain knowledge and test with cross-validation. Stepwise forward or backward also came up.
  • LLMs for features: the presenter liked the idea. The proposal: a "digital twin" of an industry expert that prepares for the real expert meeting and gives a coding agent feedback. A proposal, not something built.
🌪️
25 · Shocks
COVID-era data: neither delete it nor believe it
▼

The case: a private-equity firm looking at a home-gardening company whose sales were flat, then surged in COVID. Will demand last once people go back to work?

Neither extreme works: removing the period pretends it had no effect; treating it as the new normal assumes the surge continues. Instead:

  1. Scenarios: bound the outcome between a baseline (4% growth was one example) and higher-growth cases, with churn modeled.
  2. Cohorts: compare customers acquired in the disruption with earlier regulars on purchase patterns, demographics and churn.
  3. Ask customer-facing teams whether the new customers would have come anyway.
The question to answer

Was COVID the trigger that brought people who'd have become customers anyway, or a way to get customers unlikely to keep coming? Retention hasn't been observed yet, and a model can't learn a pattern absent from its training data.

⚖️
26 · Metrics
Judge a forecast by what its errors cost
▼

Is over- or under-forecasting worse? It depends on under-production vs overstock cost, salvage value and perishability, plus materials, labor, delivery, insurance, warehousing and lost sales. High warehousing cost pushes you to under-forecast; expensive stock-outs push the other way.

Strategy changes it too. The instructor said LVMH-type luxury brands destroy unsold stock or buy back discounted resale to protect the brand, and Stüssy uses biweekly drops and hidden quantities to create scarcity.

Out of sample means data not used to fit. In-sample R² can rise as you add features without generalizing any better.

Out-of-sample R²1 − Σ(rt − r̂t)² / Σ(rt − r̄)²

Also on the table: RMSE and absolute error. No single metric was chosen. For ROI, put money on each kind of error and report dollars saved or sales gained, not just R². A lift curve was mentioned; the recording ends before it's explained.

The lesson

One model for every product ignores that products differ: R² 0.114. A model per product fits each one (0.517). Lasso shrinks the noisy coefficients and helps further. SKU fixed effects are the middle ground: one model, but each product gets its own baseline (0.351).

Caveat: the α sweep picks the best value on the test set, as the notebook does. Strictly, tune on a validation split.

  • SKU 11 baseline: first test week forecast 43 vs actual 30. A starting point to improve on, not an accurate model.
  • The trade-off (Abbas's framing): 44 models seems excessive, but pooling lets one product distort another. Pooling helps a low-history product in a sensible category, and hurts when iPhones and MacBooks get forced together.
  • 0.517 is an average. Some SKUs may sit at 0.7–0.8 and others near 0.3, and a weak high-revenue product is still a business risk.
  • SingularMatrixWarning on some SKUs points to zero-variance or perfectly collinear features in that SKU's slice.

28 · Regularization
Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net, and why fixed effects work
▼
MethodPenalty added to OLSEffect
Lasso (L1)α Σ|βj|Drives some coefficients to exactly zero: automatic feature selection
Ridge (L2)α Σβj²Shrinks coefficients, none to zero
Elastic NetWeighted L1 + L2Both

Regularization is the concept; Lasso is one implementation. α = 0 is plain OLS. On SKU 11, Lasso kept current price, main-page promotion, trend and some months, and dropped older price lags and some vendor dummies. A dropped feature isn't proven irrelevant, especially a one-hot column.

  • Tuning α: too high drops useful features. Different SKUs carry different noise, so one global α is suboptimal: put it inside the SKU loop. Random search covers several parameters faster than a rigid grid. Spend the compute when small accuracy gains are worth real money.
  • SKU fixed effects: 44 dummy columns give each product its own intercept while slopes stay shared. Store-wide seasonality and promotions get the big sample; the intercept handles each product's baseline. Open question from class: add SKU × price slopes too?
  • Log prices: prices are right-skewed (most under $50, a few at $200+). ln(price) on own and competitor prices gets closer to symmetric and added about +0.02. Vendor and time indicators stay as they are. Square root and power transforms are other options; handle zeros first.

Class figures for SKU 11: all features ~0.309, regularized ~0.339 (my run at α = 0.5: 0.334). The class sweep figure of ~0.556 at α = 2 matches my run, but it isn't the top: mine keeps climbing to 0.602 at α = 10.

30 · Validation + Drift
Train / validation / test, and what happens after launch
▼

Tuning on the test set contaminates your benchmark. The nested split:

SetShareUse
Test30%Held out from the start; final out-of-sample score only
Validation21%Model selection (30% of the remaining 70%)
Sub-train49%Fit candidates, then refit on all 70%

With five folds, the folds come from the training data only; one fold is validation, and the test data was isolated at the start.

Model drift: macro shifts, changing preferences and fading brands decay accuracy, so you retrain on a schedule. Roles: data scientists prototype, engineer features and tune; machine learning engineers build deployment and retraining pipelines, control inference cost and track drift.

Explaining a tree, ensembles, and what order to try things
▼

Two families: linear (OLS, Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net, fixed effects, manual transforms) and tree-based (decision trees, random forests, bagging, gradient boosting such as XGBoost).

The progression

More complexity doesn't guarantee better results on small or noisy data. A student asked whether that makes neural networks the worst option; the recording ends before the answer.

32 · Clustering
The middle ground: groups of SKUs, with a fallback
▼

Between 1 global model and 44 separate ones. Start with subject-matter experts: fast movers, best sellers, subscriptions, impulse buys. Then go quantitative: aggregate to one row per SKU (average price, price standard deviation, sales volume, promotion frequency), cluster, and fit a regression per cluster.

AlgorithmHow it behaves
K-meansYou have to guess K up front. Sensitive to uneven density and non-spherical clusters
DBSCANDensity-based: finds the number of clusters itself and labels noise points

Live demo, K = 4: clusters 0, 1 and 3 took most items; cluster 2 held just 2 premium products, and they split off again at K = 2. Well-populated clusters beat the global model (about 0.10 → 0.11–0.14); the 2-product cluster did badly for lack of data.

The fallback

Use a cluster model where it helps and send the weird SKUs back to the centralized model. Choose K with the elbow method and silhouette scores, under a practical cap of about 10 models. Cold start: place a new product in a cluster from its catalog attributes and use that model.

The closing summary: "Test, measure, learn, iterate." Spend your energy linking results to business decisions rather than on algorithm names.

Reference · Day 3
Exam hooks — Day 3
▼
  • Variation beats volume: constant-price history can't show price response. A/B tests are the cleanest source of variation.
  • Sales = MIN(Demand, Inventory): sales are censored; drop stock-out weeks when estimating demand.
  • One instance proves nothing: report mean, standard deviation, confidence interval and tails over many runs; perturb forecasts (±10%).
  • Markdown = price skimming: long lead time + mixed customers. Week 1 at full price tells you the item's level; historical lift factors (1.31 / 1.73 / 2.81) give the other prices.
  • Run-out rate: pick the price mix that sells out exactly in the last week. The LP (weeks at each price, ≤ weeks left, demand ≤ stock) gives the same plan; re-optimize weekly to beat it.
  • Newsvendor: P(D ≤ X) = cu/(cu+co) = critical fractile = service level. Normal: X = μ + zσ = NORMINV(cf, μ, σ). At the optimum, one more unit is worth $0.
  • cu = price − cost; co = cost − salvage. Pumpkins 250 · T-shirts 1,114 (1,000 if price falls to $12).
  • Champagne: backlog $3 = cu, holding $1.5 = co → order-up-to 108.614, ~$32/period. (20, 100) doesn't converge ($600–$3,200); (30, 135) → $83–$87.
  • Time series: split chronologically, never randomly; sliding-window CV with windows ≥ 1 year.
  • Missing data: drop / mode-impute / model-impute / native handling / keep as a signal. Missingness is rarely random.
  • Outliers: mean ± kσ within SKU; dropping the best week can make the model fail exactly when it matters.
  • Features: trend, month dummies with drop_first, lagged prices via groupby-shift.
  • Per-product beats pooled: centralized 0.114 → decentralized 0.517 → + Lasso 0.533 (0.602 at α = 10).
  • L1 zeroes, L2 shrinks, Elastic Net does both; α = 0 is OLS. Fixed effects = own intercept, shared slopes (0.351).
  • Nested split: 49 sub-train / 21 validation / 30 test. Tuning on test contaminates the benchmark.
  • Clusters: experts first, then K-means (guess K) or DBSCAN (finds it, flags noise); fall back to the centralized model for weird SKUs.
  • Shocks: scenarios + cohorts, neither delete nor extrapolate. Metrics: price the errors; the costlier mistake sets your bias.
